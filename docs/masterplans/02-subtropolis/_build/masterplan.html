SubTropolis · A Minecraft Architecture Masterplan 01 · Executive Summary
01 · EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

An Industrial Cathedral of Utility, in 200,000 Blocks

This document is the second of five master plans in the combined-complex project (Cheyenne Mountain, SubTropolis, Houston tunnels, plus a combined report and a map-integration report). SubTropolis is a MINE, carved into a hillside — not a bunker carved into a peak. The two builds share a ravine, share a coordinate system, and differ in almost everything else.

What this build is

SubTropolis is a 200×200 block Minecraft replica of Hunt Midwest's SubTropolis — the largest underground business complex in the world, carved into 270-million-year-old Bethany Falls limestone on the bluffs above the Missouri River in Kansas City, Missouri. The real facility covers 1,100 surface acres, has 14 million square feet of leasable developed space, 8.2 miles of paved internal road, 10,000+ load-bearing limestone pillars, and 55+ tenants ranging from the United States Postal Service to the National Archives, from a data-center campus to a film archive vault. Year-round the temperature holds at 65–70°F; no weather penetrates the rock; rent runs 30–50% below comparable above-ground space.

The Minecraft build is a representative microcosm, not a literal replica. The chamber is carved into the south wall of a deep ravine that runs through the same mountain range as Cheyenne Mountain, perpendicular to it. The visitor drives into a horizontal portal in a hillside — not down a mine shaft, not up an elevator — and emerges into a vast grid of 8×8 white-painted pillars under continuous fluorescent lighting, on a paved two-lane street with a 15 mph speed limit and a stop sign at every intersection. The 350-block main avenue is named Hushpuckney, after a local rock type.

Why it matters

The build is a working hypothesis about the difference between a bunker and a mine. Cheyenne Mountain (build 01) is a defensive installation carved into a granite peak; the architecture is what was put in to protect. SubTropolis is a repurposed commercial facility carved into a limestone bluff; the architecture is what was left after the rock was taken out. The same mountain range, two different design philosophies, two different aesthetic languages. The combined-complex project treats them as the two poles of an axis — defensive vs. civilian, additive vs. subtractive, classified vs. public.

The 6 binding decisions

D1 — Scale. 200×200 block footprint, 1:1 vertical (5-block ceiling, 8×8 pillars, 12-block corridors), ~350-block main avenue, two ~100-block side spurs. No “big cavern” rooms.

D2 — Must-haves in 3 tiers. Tier 1 (load-bearing): horizontal portal, pillar grid, second portal. Tier 2 (signature): geology street names, painted pillar numbers, 15 mph + stop signs, 6+ distinct tenants, ghost mine. Tier 3 (flavor): data center, climate feel, surface plateau with Hunt/Chiefs plaque.

D3 — Source conflicts resolved by hierarchy. 55 M sq ft (marquee) over 14 M (leasing office). 16 ft (5 blocks at 1:1) ceiling. 150 ft depth, “up to 160 ft” as the max. 55+ tenants. 1964 founding. 68°F (65–70°F range). 50–70% energy savings (not the 90% marketing number).

D4 — Creative license legend. Every element tagged [D] documented, [I] inferred, or [X] invented. Invention standard: match a documented tenant's actual business activity, follow one of four fit-out archetypes (records archive, package logistics, data center, general industrial), and be “operationally plausible.”

D5 — Easter eggs as places, not posters. Prominent: USPS dock scene, NARA archive, STC data center, EPA office, the “World's Largest Underground Business Complex”® trademark. Subtle: UV&S labeled film cans, Hunt Hall round room, FTZ sign, ENERGY STAR 100 certificate, shift-change signs, employee cafeteria. Out: Indiana Jones, Batcave, Bond villain, torches, mob spawners, fantasy.

D6 — Combined-complex fictions. Service tunnel to Cheyenne (6×5 blocks, sub-basement, minecart-compatible). Public shaft to the city (5×5 blocks, vertical, with a Public Access Lobby). Both tagged [X] in this master plan and the combined-complex report.

SubTropolis · A Minecraft Architecture Masterplan 02 · History & Research
02 · HISTORY & RESEARCH

From 1940s Limestone Mine to the World's Largest Underground Business Complex

SubTropolis is not a designed facility. It is a 1940s limestone mine that was empty, and that a sports family with a real-estate arm decided in 1964 to lease as warehouse space. The 1960s industrial aesthetic is a consequence, not a choice. The 10,000 pillars and 8.2 miles of road are what the room-and-pillar mining method left behind.

The 1940s — original mining era

The site that became SubTropolis was first developed in 1945 as a lateral limestone-mining operation east of present-day I-435 in Clay County, Missouri, by Midwest Precote, a regional asphalt producer. The rock was used to pave Kansas City roads and airport tarmacs. Mining continued through the end of the 20th century; the resulting voids are what now house the complex. The roof rock is competent, dry, and self-supporting over very large spans — the mined stratum is described as “nearly level and very deep.”

1964 — the Hunt Midwest pivot

Lamar Hunt — better known as the founder of the AFL, owner of the Kansas City Chiefs, and principal founder of Major League Soccer — was a chief investor in the Great Midwest Corporation, which purchased Midwest Precote in 1970 to develop emptied mining sites for commercial use. Before that, Hunt had already personally “pulled the trigger” on SubTropolis in 1964, with the first underground complex opening for commercial leasing that same year, initially for “good and equipment storage.”

The Hunt family remains one of America's wealthiest dynasties, with marquee entities that include the Kansas City Chiefs, the Chicago Bulls, FC Dallas, the United Center, and Hunt Midwest. An ESPN documentary, The Kingdom (Sept. 2025), covers Hunt's role in founding SubTropolis, with a feature article in Axios Local Kansas City (Feb 2026) describing the annual “Groundhog Run” charity race held inside the mine.

The climate, the strangest feature

⚡ The killer feature The 18,000–24,000 psi compressive strength of the Bethany Falls limestone (roughly 6× structural concrete) makes the rock envelope a 6×-concrete thermal mass. SubTropolis · A Minecraft Architecture Masterplan 03 · The Soul of the Place
03 · THE SOUL

Industrial Cathedral of Utility

SubTropolis is a working industrial city that happens to be inside a mountain, and the entire emotional payload of the place comes from the collision between those two facts. The build's job is to deliver utilitarian awe: the strange, slightly disorienting wonder of realizing that a fluorescent-lit, painted-concrete, dock-doored, forklift-busy city is hiding in plain sight under a field in the middle of America, and that the boringness is the point.

The column grid is the philosophical thesis

The pillar grid is the single most iconic image of SubTropolis, and it is the inverse of every other underground facility in the project. The argument:

  • Cheyenne Mountain's signature is its blast doors. Human-made, defensive, additive. The architecture is what was put in to protect.
  • SubTropolis's signature is its pillars. Human-removed, structural, subtractive. The architecture is what was left after the rock was taken out.
  • The pillars are the permanent architecture. The 1964 construction company is gone. Ford's 25-acre vehicle lease is gone. Pillsbury and Russell Stover have moved in and out. The pillars remain. In 2080, the same pillars will hold up the ceiling while a different set of tenants moves in.
  • The pillars also say something about engineering philosophy. In a bunker, you over-build the protective envelope. In a mine-turned-warehouse, you over-build the left-in-place structure. Both are forms of paranoia; one is defensive (what if they attack?), the other is industrial (what if the ceiling falls?). The SubTropolis pillars are the answer to the industrial question.
SubTropolis vs. Cheyenne in 1 sentence

Cheyenne says security. SubTropolis says permanence. The design team should treat the pillar grid as the equivalent of the Cheyenne blast doors — the visual signature that says “this place, not that place.”

The 3 most iconic features

#1

The horizontal portal in a hillside

Trucks drive in. No elevators, no vertical shaft, no pit. The portal is the operational fact that makes the facility viable, and it is the single most-memorable visual moment.

#2

The pillar grid

8×8 white-painted pillars on 65-block centers. The repetition is the impression. The inverse of Cheyenne's blast doors. The defining image.

#3

The 15 mph speed limit + Hushpuckney street sign

The detail that most signals “this is a real place, not a generic cave.” The street names are repeatedly cited; the speed limit is the other canonical detail.

The operations manager's perspective

The daily rhythm of SubTropolis is not the rhythm of a cathedral or a fortress. It is the rhythm of a working logistics facility: a security guard waving a truck driver through at 06:00, a forklift squeaking on a polished floor at 07:30, dock doors thudding open at 08:00, the soft hum of HVAC over a 12-hour shift, the fluorescent lights never changing, a shift change at 14:00, the cafeteria coffee getting refilled at 14:15, the dim amber lights over the records archive staying on all night because the climate never changes, the security golf cart rolling past at 22:00, the ghost-mine side chamber staying dark and cold because the mining crew is somewhere else today. That is the build's heartbeat. The visitor should feel it without being told.

The paradox — tiny surface, vast underground

The exterior is the most underrated architectural statement of the place. A 55-million-sq-ft facility with a single small surface building. Almost invisible. The Hunt Midwest leasing office is a small modern building set into a tree-covered ravine wall, with a paved road, a security gate, and a parking lot. The mine is below. The world above is unaware.

The paradox: the largest underground business complex in the world is, from the surface, almost nothing. That is the design feature. If the surface building were a monument, it would be wrong. The fact that you could drive past it without realizing what was under your feet is the point.

The visitor experience — stage by stage

The build is choreographed as a staged experience. The Minecraft visitor is a player walking or driving through the build, not a tourist — so each stage specifies what the player sees, the light level, the ambient sound, and the block underfoot.

1

Drive-up (the Kansas City plain)

Rolling plains to the south, grass/dirt, scattered trees, full daylight, surface ambient. The small modern building is visible against the ravine wall. This is a normal surface environment. The portal is unremarkable.

2

Surface entrance

The visitor center + parking lot, a security booth, the portal mouth visible against the south face. Visible: the “SubTropolis” marquee sign, tenant directory, “SPEED LIMIT 15,” clearance warning. Still surface, but the scale is wrong. The portal is bigger than expected. The bluff is taller than expected.

3

The descent (the canonical moment)

The paved two-lane road slopes gently down into the hillside. The sky disappears within 30–50 blocks. Fluorescent strip lighting becomes visible at the bottom. The light changes from full daylight to a cooler, dimmer ambient over the descent. The transition is clean and immediate — the visitor knows within 10 blocks they are underground.

4

The arrival (the pillar grid reveal)

The bottom of the ramp, where the road levels out at the south edge of the main grid. The first major intersection is visible: a 3×3 grid of 8×8 white-painted pillars extending in every direction. Fluorescent overhead. A “Hushpuckney Ave” street sign. The wow moment. Disproportionate design effort should go here.

5

Main avenues

The visitor walks or drives down a 200-block main avenue, lined with pillar after pillar. Tenant fit-outs visible on either side. The avenue is straight; the pillars are evenly spaced; the ceiling is flat. The repetition is the impression. The second iconic moment, after the reveal. The “this is bigger than I expected” feeling.

6

Tenant zone visits

USPS loading dock, NARA archive, STC data center, EPA office, Hallmark / Russell Stover, Grainger, UV&S film vault, Hunt Hall, Ford/Grainger vehicle bays. Each is visually distinct but built on the same rock shell. Variety within consistency.

7

The ghost mine

A side corridor blocked by a barricade with “DANGER — ACTIVE MINING.” Beyond: dark, raw rock, no lights, a parked mine cart. The contrast between developed and undeveloped. Most of the 55 M sq ft is unfinished.

8

Climate-controlled vault zone

NARA archive and UV&S film vault. Dim amber lighting. Climate-control signage. Archive boxes. Film cans. The “this is why tenants rent here” feeling. Constant climate = archival value.

9

Public Access Lobby (combined-complex exit)

A small lobby with a security guard booth, a turnstile, and a visible vertical shaft going up. Sign reads “PUBLIC TRANSIT — COMBINED COMPLEX.” The next stop is the city (via the public shaft) or Cheyenne (via the service tunnel at the NW sub-basement).

10

Exit portal

The visitor turns back to the main avenue, drives east, ascends the exit ramp, and emerges through the second portal onto the east face of the hill. The daylight returns. The drive-through is complete. From the east face, looking back, the SubTropolis hill is unremarkable — a small surface building, a parking lot, a tree-covered bluff.

⚡ The dramatic tension The most SubTropolis-flavored visual in the build is not a dramatic moment; it is the moment when the player realizes they have been walking for 200 blocks and the pillars still haven't changed. That is the iconic image. The drama is in the repetition.

05 · THE DELIBERATION

How a 4-Person Panel Decided What SubTropolis Should Be

Before the design team could place a block, four voices argued what the build should be: a Realist who wanted only what was documented, a Gameplay Advocate who wanted it traversable, an Architect who wanted one unified composition, and an Operations Manager who wanted it to feel like a place where people actually work. They disagreed in the right ways, and the decisions below reflect that disagreement resolved.

Topic 1 — Scale compression

At 1 block = 1 meter, the real facility is unbuildable. What compresses?

RealistVertical 1:1 is the only dimension that absolutely cannot be touched, because the 16-ft ceiling, the 8×8 pillars, and the 65-ft pillar-to-pillar spacing are the visual identity. Compress floor area aggressively.
Gameplay Advocate200×200 is fine on paper but in Minecraft that's a 4-minute sprint. The 300–400-block main avenue has to feel like a single drive-through, not a slog.
ArchitectThe 200×200 is the right footprint because of the 8×8 pillar geometry. The length of the main avenue should run the long axis of the available ravine wall.
Operations ManagerThe question is whether a visitor feels the scale of the place without having to walk it. Sight lines: a player at one end should be able to see pillars and ceiling lights fading into haze at the other end.
Decision (binding): ~200×200 block footprint, 1:1 vertical (5-block ceiling, 8×8 pillars, 12-block corridors), ~350-block main avenue along the long axis of the ravine wall, two ~100-block side spurs. Compression ratio ~1:100 floor, 1:1 vertical. No "big cavern" rooms.

Topic 2 — Must-haves vs. cuts (3 tiers)

The research lists 12 must-haves. Which 8–12 are binding?

RealistEvery item on the research list is publicly documented. I would not cut any. If the build has to drop one, it should be the rail spur — it's real, but it's secondary.
Gameplay AdvocateEvery must-have should be either a visual or a navigation hook. Surface parking and rail spur demoted. The data center, USPS loading dock, NARA archive are the anchors; they need to be fully realized.
ArchitectThe pillar grid is the silhouette. If you remove it, you have a generic underground warehouse. I'd organize must-haves in three tiers: load-bearing, signature, flavor.
Operations ManagerA place where people work has docks. SubTropolis has 500+ truck docks. A build that doesn't have visible dock doors is a build that doesn't respect the operation.
Decision (binding): Depth-over-coverage. The pillar grid + two portals + tenant variety + the unfinished ghost chamber form the irreducible core. Cuts: full surface parking, the 2,500 surface acres, the amusement park, the full 10.5 mi of road, the 7.5 M sq ft of expansion capacity, the Ford 25-acre lease as a named exhibit. Faded references: one above-ground block, one plaque in Hunt Hall, one FTZ sign.

Topic 3 — Source conflicts

Six real conflicts: total area, ceiling height, depth, tenant count, opening date, temperature.

RealistPick the most-cited and most-corroborated figure for each. 55 M sq ft, 160 ft deep, 16 ft ceiling, 55+ tenants, 1964, 68°F.
Gameplay AdvocateI don't care which numbers are "true" — I care which numbers make the build feel right. 55 M is the famous number. Use 55 M. The 14 M can appear on a "FLOOR AREA LEASED" sign inside the leasing office.
ArchitectThe numbers should be deployed in service of composition. The number at the entrance is 55 M — the claim. The number inside the leasing office is 14 M — the fact. The number on the deepest-chamber sign is 150 ft.
Operations ManagerMy leasing team would quote 55 M of mined-out and 14 M of developed. The 55 M is the headline; the 14 M is what I send a contract with.
Decision (binding): Marketing claim at the threshold, technical fact in the back office, variant in the master plan only. See §2 Table 2.2 for the full hierarchy. The 55 M vs 14 M distinction is the same distinction the Atlantic, the ULI study, and Wikipedia all make.

Topic 4 — Creative license boundaries ([D]/[I]/[X])

Most SubTropolis tenant interiors are private. Where is invention allowed?

RealistDefault to "do not invent." Mark every invention with a small italicized "designer interpretation" note in the master plan; do not mark individual builds (the player doesn't need to know which is which).
Gameplay AdvocateThe player doesn't read the master plan. As long as the interior feel matches what an industrial storage facility looks like, the player will believe it. Invent freely, but invent with conviction.
ArchitectVisual coherence demands a design language. Four archetypes: long-term records archive (NARA), package logistics (USPS), data center (STC), general industrial (Hallmark, Russell Stover). Each archetype has a defined palette and a representative interior.
Operations ManagerThe way to invent a tenant interior is to ask: "what does this tenant do all day?" Invent the activity, not just the objects.
Decision (binding): Master-plan legend: [D] documented, [I] inferred from industry standard, [X] invented. Invention standard: match documented business activity, follow one of 4 fit-out archetypes, be "operationally plausible."

Topic 5 — Easter eggs (places, not posters)

SubTropolis is associated with USPS, NARA, the climate, the Hunt/Chiefs family. Which easter eggs?

RealistThree rules: (1) no torches, no idols, no treasure, no hidden lair imagery — Indiana Jones contamination is out; (2) easter eggs must be referenced in the documented history; (3) the Hunt/Chiefs reference is allowed because Lamar Hunt is the documented founder.
Gameplay AdvocatePlayers love easter eggs. The Hunt/Chiefs reference should be visible but tasteful — a single Chiefs-color accent on a sign, a "Hunt Hall" round room, a Lamar Hunt bust.
ArchitectEaster eggs must compose. A Chiefs helmet on a wall is a poster, not architecture. A Lamar Hunt bronze in a small round room is a place. Make the easter eggs a small number of named places rather than a sprinkling of hidden references.
Operations ManagerThe things that aren't in any press release are the things the workers remember: the constant hum, the smell of cardboard, the security guard, the truck driver radio, the morning shift queue, the cafeteria. Those are the easter eggs I want.
Decision (binding): Prominent: USPS dock scene, NARA archive, STC data center, EPA, the "®" trademark. Subtle: UV&S labeled film cans, Hunt Hall round room, FTZ sign, ENERGY STAR 100, shift-change signs, employee cafeteria, Groundhog Run banner, 2001 USPS anthrax plaque. Out: Indiana Jones, Batcave, Bond villain, torches, fake idols, mob spawners, fantasy contamination, movie posters, helmet imagery.

Topic 6 — Combined-complex integration

The entrance ramp — the canonical visitor moment

A 1970s industrial experience, not a Bond villain's drawbridge. The entrance ramp is the single most important visitor moment. The design team should plan the visual to be a ~50-block descent with a gentle grade (≤ 5%) so semi-trucks can use it.

  • Length: ~50–100 blocks (at ≤ 5% grade; the exact path is the architect's discretion — a straight diagonal is acceptable, or a gentle S-curve).
  • Width: 12 blocks of drivable road, with 2-block shoulders of stone-brick on each side.
  • Ceiling: lowers from open sky to the rock ceiling gradually over the first 20 blocks of the descent, then closes fully.
  • Wall treatment: rough smooth stone + calcite (the limestone blend) for the first 20 blocks, transitioning to white concrete for the interior finish over the last 30 blocks. The transition is the “you are now in the painted corridor” moment.
  • Floor: polished andesite with yellow concrete center line and white concrete edge lines.

Carve First, Then Build

The SubTropolis build is subtractive before it is additive: the place is a hole in a hill, not a building on flat ground. The construction sequence must therefore begin with terrain, then carve the chamber, then build the contents. Trying to construct the tenant zones before the chamber exists means placing blocks in mid-air, fighting gravity, and then re-shaping the room around them.

Build strategy

Why carve-first

Predicable chamber, then fit-outs

Carving first gives a clean, flat, sealed chamber with predictable coordinates. The tenant fit-outs can be assembled as additions rather than foundations.

Why not schematic-first

Mixed: regular shell, irregular fit-outs

200×200 with 30+ tenant fit-outs is too irregular to schematic in one pass. Schematic the regular parts (chamber shell, pillar grid, main avenue) and in-place-build the irregular parts (tenant fit-outs, signage, easter eggs).

Why not bot-first

Hybrid: bot for big, human for judgment

Bot tooling for the big regular operations (chamber carving, pillar grid). Human or tightly-scripted bot for tenant fit-outs and easter eggs.

The 7 construction phases

3. Coordinate System

World origin (0, 70, 0) is the main entrance portal at surface level on the south face of the SubTropolis hill. Y=70 is just above sea level (sea level = Y=64). North = −Z (the mine extends into negative Z; the ravine is further negative Z). East = +X. Up = +Y.

Build direction. The main grid is built from the south portal inward (Z = 0 → Z = −200), then side spurs and tenant fit-outs branch off. The sub-basement is built after the main grid floor is in place (it's accessed from the main grid). Y compression is 1:1 — the 5-block ceiling is exactly 5 blocks tall, the 8×8 pillars are exactly 8×8, the 12-block corridors are exactly 12 blocks wide. Do not compress these dimensions.
