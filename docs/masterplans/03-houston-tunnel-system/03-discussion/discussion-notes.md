# Houston Tunnel System — Discussion Notes

> **Discussion Moderator's deliberation record for the Houston tunnel-system Minecraft replica build.**
> Documented panel: Realist, Gameplay Advocate, Urban Planner, Office Worker.
> Outcome: binding decisions for the Site Planner, Architectural Designer, and AI Contractor Writer.
> Companion to `01-research/research-report.md`, `01-research/references.md`, and `02-visuals/visual-assets.md`.

---

## 1. The Panel

This deliberation decides what a Minecraft replica of the Houston downtown tunnel system should *be*. The real system is unrenderable at any literal scale: 95+ city blocks, six miles of corridor, 80+ access points through private office-building lobbies, two direct street-level entries (Wells Fargo Plaza and McKinney Garage), one iconic food-court archetype (the Esperson), and a daily rhythm of 200,000 office workers who treat it as their climate-controlled commute between building lobbies and lunch counters. The above-ground city is a *real* city — Houston's CBD, with its named skywalk network running parallel to the tunnels one story up. Both layers have to be built, and the transition between them has to be the design's most important decision. Four voices argued that question: a **Realist** who wants only what's publicly documented and is allergic to subway-and-mall contamination; a **Gameplay Advocate** who wants a build a Minecraft player will actually walk from end to end; an **Urban Planner** who wants the city above and the layer below to read as one place; and an **Office Worker** who has actually commuted through the real tunnels and knows the lunch rush, the lock-up, the chain restaurants, the Wells Fargo direct-entry hack, and the climate feel. They disagreed in the right ways, and the decisions below reflect that disagreement resolved.

## 2. Deliberation Transcript

### Topic 1 — Scale Compression

**The question.** At 1:1 (1 block = 1 meter), the build is 95+ city blocks and 6 miles of corridor — roughly 9,700 blocks of walkway, or a 38-minute continuous walk. The research suggests a "6–12 city blocks" sample as a realistic target. The Cameron Management 2015 detailed map (`schematics/buildings-streets-tunnels-detailed-page01.png`) gives a labeled grid of every connected building (A-15 through P-29). What compression does the final build actually use, and what shape does the sample take?

**Realist.** The Cameron 2015 map is the gift here. We have an *actual* grid of every connected building, with named segments — Downtown Tunnel Loop, W. Walker, N. Louisiana, N. Travis, Lamar, E. McKinney. That map is the truth of the system, and the build should respect its *taxonomy* even when the *footprint* is compressed. I will accept aggressive compression of linear dimensions — Houston's CBD block is roughly 80 m × 80 m; 4:1 linear compression to a 20 m × 20 m "build block" is fine. What I will not accept is a generic blob. The named segments must be the *named segments*. The 4×6 block sample I am about to defend should preserve at least five of the six documented named routes. And the vertical — 20 ft below grade — must hold at 1:1, because the *squeeze* between street and tunnel ceiling is the experience.

**Gameplay Advocate.** A 4×6 block sample is 24 city blocks. If each compressed block is 20 m × 20 m, the build footprint is 80 m × 120 m, or 9,600 blocks². That's about 2–3 minutes of walking corner to corner, which is the right range for a player to *explore the whole thing on one visit*. I am pulling hard against a 6×8 (48 blocks) sample — that becomes a 4-minute walk between corners and the corners die. 4×6 with one major food court per quadrant is the call. The linear compression of corridor (10–20 ft real → 10–20 ft compressed, basically 1:1 on corridor dimensions) is the *only* dimension that should be 1:1; the building footprints get 4:1. And vertical 1:1 is fine because the tunnel is only 1–2 blocks deep.

**Urban Planner.** I want to talk about *shape*. A 4×6 grid can be drawn two ways: a 4-block-wide × 6-block-tall portrait, or a 6-wide × 4-tall landscape. Houston's downtown is oriented *north–south along Main/Fannin* with named east–west streets (Texas, Capitol, Rusk, McKinney, Dallas, Lamar) crossing them. The 6-tall × 4-wide orientation is the Houston orientation — the long axis runs N–S along the real street grid. That preserves the actual street names as readable labels in the build: Main, Fannin, Travis, Louisiana, Walker, Capitol on the long axis, with McKinney, Rusk, Dallas, Lamar as cross streets. A 4×6 in the *wrong* orientation is Houston rotated 90°, which the Office Worker will notice immediately. Get the orientation right.

**Office Worker.** Forget the block count for a second. I have walked this system, and I have never walked all of it. I have walked *my route* — lobby, down the escalator, through the basement corridor, into the Esperson food court, lunch, back. Maybe three or four specific buildings' basements, in a rotation. The build doesn't need to render 95 blocks because nobody walks 95 blocks. It needs to render *four or five distinct daily routes* with enough detail that I can recognize my own commute in the build. I will accept a 4×6 grid if and only if the four quadrants each contain one of the documented food courts (Esperson, Pennzoil, 1001 Fannin/Lamar, McKinney) and the routes between them feel like *commutes*, not hallways. If you give me four food courts and the corridors that connect them, that's Houston. If you give me 24 generic blocks with four food courts scattered randomly, that's a mall.

**Tension points.**
- The Realist wants the named-segment taxonomy preserved. The Urban Planner wants the orientation to match Houston's actual CBD grid (Main/Fannin N–S). The Gameplay Advocate wants the footprint small enough to be walkable. The Office Worker wants the four food courts to be the *anchors*, not the corridors.
- The Realist and Office Worker are closer than they look — both want a sample that *reads* like the real system, not a generic underground mall.
- The Gameplay Advocate and Urban Planner are also aligned — both want the build navigable and the city above to read as a real city.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The four perspectives agree more than they disagree. The disagreement is on *shape* (4×6 portrait vs. 6×4 landscape), *orientation* (Houston N–S axis), and *what the build anchors on* (food courts as the four nodes). The binding call: a **6-block N–S × 4-block E–W** sample (24 compressed blocks, ~80 m × 120 m, ~9,600 blocks²) with the long axis running along the real Houston street grid. The four food courts anchor the four quadrants. Linear compression on building footprint is 4:1; corridor cross-section is 1:1; vertical is 1:1 (tunnel sits 6 blocks below grade, exactly as the real system does).

**Decision (binding).**
- **Sample footprint:** 6 blocks N–S × 4 blocks E–W = 24 compressed city blocks (~80 m × 120 m at 4:1 linear compression).
- **Long axis orientation:** N–S along Main / Fannin / Travis / Louisiana / Walker / Capitol. E–W cross streets: Rusk, McKinney, Dallas, Lamar.
- **Linear compression:** 4:1 on city block footprint. 1:1 on tunnel corridor cross-section (10–20 ft width, 8–9 ft ceiling per research §4.3) and food-court node width (30–40 ft).
- **Vertical compression:** 1:1. Tunnel sits 6 blocks below street grade (20 ft). The street-level layer and the tunnel layer are both visible and traversable.
- **Compression ratio:** roughly **1:4** by block count (95+ → 24), **1:1** by corridor cross-section, **1:1** vertical, **1:4** by linear length (6 mi → ~1.5 mi of corridor in the sample).
- **Named-segment preservation:** at least 5 of the 6 documented named segments are represented in the sample (Downtown Tunnel Loop, N. Louisiana, N. Travis, Lamar, E. McKinney). The Cameron 2015 grid labels (A-15 through P-29) are used as the *build-block identifiers* in the master plan.
- **Effective player time from Wells Fargo entry to the farthest food court (McKinney):** target 2–3 minutes of walking, with the corridors deliberately broken up by short ramps, signage, and building-lobby transitions so the journey feels longer than the distance.

### Topic 2 — Must-Haves vs. Cuts

**The question.** The research lists 13 iconic features in §11 ("What a Minecraft Visitor Should Recognize"). The panel must commit to 8–12 *binding* must-haves, name what gets cut entirely, and identify what gets a "faded reference."

**Realist.** Every item on the research list is publicly documented. The descent from a 1970s office-tower lobby, the Esperson food court, the Pennzoil Place underground, the Wells Fargo direct entry, the McKinney Garage concourse, the 1001 Fannin / Lamar food court, the skybridge, the "Tunnel" backlit sign, the standard corridor (dropped ceiling, painted concrete, fluorescent, VCT floor), the lunch rush, the after-hours shutdown, the floodgate, the disconnected section (Harris County or Theater District) — all 13 are *the place*. I will fight to keep the skybridge because the Houston system is famous for *both* tunnel and skywalk, and dropping the skywalk makes the tunnel system look like a stand-alone underground mall, not Houston. I will fight to keep the disconnected section because the holes in the map are the truth of the piecemeal ownership story.

**Gameplay Advocate.** Thirteen features at full fidelity is a museum. The build has to be *traversable* in 20–30 minutes of focused play, and every feature has to earn its visit. The features that pay rent: (1) the descent (entry moment), (2) one iconic food court in full detail (Esperson as the archetype), (3) the Wells Fargo entry (the famous direct access), (4) the McKinney concourse (the *other* public entry), (5) the 1001 Fannin / Lamar food court (the modern refresh, contrasts Esperson), (6) the standard corridor (the dominant visual), (7) the skybridge (above-ground companion to the tunnel), (8) the floodgate (the hidden engineering detail). That's 8 binding must-haves. I will cut: a separate "lunch rush" build (it's embedded in the food court; we render the food court at peak and that's the lunch rush), the Harris County tunnel as a full build (one sign is enough), and the full multi-tenant list (pick 10 representative tenants, fade the rest).

**Urban Planner.** The must-haves organize into three tiers. **Tier 1 — load-bearing:** the descent, the four food courts (one per quadrant), and the two direct street-level entries (Wells Fargo and McKinney). Without these, the build is not a Houston tunnel. **Tier 2 — signature:** the standard corridor (the dominant visual), the skybridge (the above-ground companion), the "Tunnel" backlit wayfinding signage, the floodgate. **Tier 3 — flavor:** the after-hours shutdown (lights-off moment), the disconnected section (one sign), the lunch rush (one dense food-court scene). I will fight to keep the *city above* — at least one skybridge crossing over a real street — because without the above-ground moment the tunnel layer reads as a separate game.

**Office Worker.** I'll tell you what I actually see. I see a glass revolving door at Wells Fargo, an elevator bank, an interior stair down, a long corridor with a dropped ceiling, the Esperson food court on my left with Chick-fil-A on the corner, the "Tunnel → Pennzoil Place" sign, the corridor narrows, a ramp up to the Pennzoil lobby, two Philip Johnson mirrored towers above my head visible through the lobby glass, back down to the tunnel, the "Tunnel → 1001 Fannin" sign, the corridor widens at the Lamar Tunnel food court, Blackwater Coffee, Charlies BBQ, lunch. I see the *daily commute*. The build has to render *that* commute, not a generic mall. The features that match my daily: the descent, the four food courts, the standard corridor, the wayfinding signs, the skybridge, the Wells Fargo direct entry, the *fluorescent light smell* (the Office Worker is a visual advocate but the *humidity* and the *quiet* are part of the feel). Cut: the Harris County tunnel (I have never used it; it's a separate system for courthouse workers), the lunch rush as a separate build (any food court at peak *is* the lunch rush), and the Tropical Storm Allison detail (one small marker, not a flooded corridor scene).

**Tension points.**
- The Realist wants all 13; the Gameplay Advocate wants 8. The Office Worker wants 7 of the Gameplay Advocate's 8 plus the after-hours shutdown (which the Gameplay Advocate listed as a "faded" feature). The Urban Planner wants the city above to be a tier-1 must-have, not just the skybridge.
- The Realist and Office Worker agree the floodgate is a must-have, but disagree on whether to render a flooded corridor scene (Office Worker says no, Realist hedges). The Realist's *citation* instinct pulls toward documenting Allison; the Office Worker's *feel* instinct pulls toward the daily routine.
- The Urban Planner's "city above" argument is the strongest objection to the Gameplay Advocate's minimalism. A tunnel-only build is a mall; a tunnel-plus-skybridge build is Houston.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The Realist is right that the public record supports 13 features; the Gameplay Advocate is right that a 13-feature build is a museum. The resolution is *hierarchy*: 8 tier-1 must-haves get full build, 4 tier-2 features get either one iconic instance or a faded reference, and the rest are documented in the master plan but not built. The Office Worker's daily commute is the *narrative spine* — the four food courts, the standard corridor, the two public entries, the descent, and the wayfinding. The Realist's floodgate is a tier-1 because it's the only engineering detail that distinguishes the Houston tunnel from a generic underground concourse. The Urban Planner's skybridge is tier-1 because the build is a *two-layer city*, not just a tunnel.

### Topic 3 — Source Conflicts

**The question.** The research flags five real conflicts: block count (95 vs. 95+ vs. 100+), tunnel length (6 mi vs. 7 mi), daily users (150,000 vs. 200,000 vs. 250,000+), founding date (1930s vs. 1935), and depth (20 ft vs. 30 ft). What does the build canon use?

**Realist.** I will defend the most-cited, most-corroborated figure for each, and I will defend the *dual* origin story because it is documented. Block count: 95+ (Wikipedia, downtownhouston.org, Houston Chronicle, City Cast, Casiola, Secret Houston, HTXTrip, 365things, Houston Public Media — all agree; the "100+" figure is older, less precise). Tunnel length: 6 mi (consensus across all major sources; AMLI's "7 mi" is the lone outlier and the research explicitly flags it as such). Daily users: 200,000 (most recent consensus; 150,000 is older and 250,000+ is aspirational). Founding: 1930s, *with* the dual origin (Ross Sterling 1931 under Fannin, Will Horwitz 1935 under Capitol) — this is the public record and the build honors both with two plaques or one combined plaque. Depth: 20 ft (consensus; 30 ft is the depth-to-basement-floor variant, not the tunnel-floor-to-grade distance — the AMLI source explicitly clarifies this).

**Gameplay Advocate.** I care less about the number and more about what the number *does* in the build. 200,000 daily users is a great stat for a sign at the entry — it's a *moment*. 95+ is a great stat because the "+" leaves room for the player's imagination. 6 miles is fine; 7 miles is fine; the player will never notice the difference and we don't need a sign that says "6 mi exactly" anywhere except a single interpretive panel. Founding 1930s is more evocative than 1935 because the decade feels like a *thing* (the Depression, Rockefeller Center, the original tunnels). Depth 20 ft is the right call because the 1:1 vertical depends on it.

**Urban Planner.** I am interested in the numbers that *shape the city above*. The 95+ block count determines the *city footprint* (a CBD footprint, not a suburban one). The 200,000 daily users justifies the *density* of the above-ground city — the city is dense because 200,000 people are working in it, and the tunnel system exists because the city is dense. The 20 ft depth is the *physical relationship* between street level and tunnel level — the descent is short, one story down, and that determines the lobby-to-corridor transition. The founding date and the tunnel length don't shape the city above; they're historical color.

**Office Worker.** The numbers I recognize from my daily life: 95+ blocks (yes, that's about right), 6 miles (sounds right; I never measured), 200,000 (plausible; the lunch rush at Esperson is shoulder-to-shoulder), 1930s (yes, the origin is old, the system feels old), 20 ft (yes, the descent is one story; it feels like a basement). The 150,000 figure feels low; 250,000+ feels like a press release. 30 ft would be wrong — the tunnel is one story, not two. The "1935 only" origin feels wrong — the system has the Sterling tunnel and the Horwitz tunnel and they are different. Pick the consensus number on each, and on the founding date, tell the dual story.

**Tension points.**
- The Realist and Office Worker agree on the consensus numbers for almost everything. The only real tension is the daily user count: 200,000 is the consensus, but 250,000+ has appeared in some recent sources. The Office Worker says 250,000+ "feels like a press release." The Realist defers to recent sources.
- The dual-origin story (Sterling + Horwitz) is not really a tension; the Office Worker and Realist both endorse it. The only question is whether to render two separate plaques or one combined one — a design-stage decision, not a discussion-stage one.
- The 7 mi outlier is the easiest call: 6 mi is the consensus and AMLI's 7 mi is explicitly flagged in the research.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The conflicts resolve cleanly. The Realist's consensus picks are correct on every count. The 200,000 daily users stat is the *most* important number for the build because it is the public-facing stat the Office Worker recognizes from real life. The dual-origin story is a Houston-specific detail (most cities have a single founding story; Houston has two simultaneous ones) and the build honors it with one combined plaque at the entry. The 20 ft depth holds because the 1:1 vertical depends on it and the AMLI clarification rules out the 30 ft variant.

**Decision (binding).**
- **Block count: 95+.** All signs, panels, and master-plan documents use the phrase "95+ city blocks" (per downtownhouston.org phrasing). The "+" is honored.
- **Tunnel length: 6 mi.** Used on the "95+ blocks / 6 mi / 200,000 daily users" interpretive panel at the Wells Fargo entry. The 7 mi outlier is *not* used.
- **Daily users: 200,000.** Used on the interpretive panel. The 150,000 and 250,000+ variants are *not* used in any player-facing signage.
- **Founding: 1930s, dual origin.** One combined interpretive plaque at the Wells Fargo entry reads: "Houston Tunnel System — Established 1930s. First tunnel: Ross Sterling under Fannin (1931) and Will Horwitz under Capitol (1935). Inspired by the Rockefeller Center underground concourse."
- **Depth: 20 ft.** Used on the interpretive panel and as the canonical vertical compression ratio. The 30 ft variant is *not* used (it is the depth-to-basement-floor, not tunnel-floor-to-grade, and the AMLI source clarifies this).
- **Master plan numbers (binding for downstream):** 95+ blocks, 6 mi, 200,000 daily users, 1930s, 20 ft below grade, ~72 °F year-round inside the tunnel.

### Topic 4 — Creative License Boundaries

**The question.** Most tenant interiors are private. The Cameron 2015 map shows building footprints and named segments but not interiors. The Esperson food court is the most-documented zone; everything else is partially-documented or undocumented. Where is creative license allowed? How do we mark "real layout" vs. "designer's interpretation" in the master plan?

**Realist.** The Cameron 2015 map gives us the *real* taxonomy: building footprints, tunnel segments, the named-segment grid. That map is `[D]`. Anything inside a building footprint that is not on the Cameron map is *invented* and must be tagged `[X]`. I will accept one class of inference: a tenant whose business is documented (e.g., "Chick-fil-A — Pennzoil Place underground") can have an interior that follows the documented Chick-fil-A prototype (counter line, menu board, ordering queue, dining perimeter). That is `[I]` (inferred from industry archetype), not `[X]`. The standard of invention is: if a former downtown Houston office worker walked in, they would not laugh. The Esperson food court is the bar; everything else has to clear the Esperson bar.

**Gameplay Advocate.** I am less interested in tagging than in *coherence*. If half the build is `[D]` and half is `[X]` and the player cannot tell which is which, the tagging has failed. The standard is: the player should not be able to tell the difference between a documented zone and an invented one. The Cameron map is the *layout* truth; the tenant interiors are *inferred* from industry archetype for the named tenants and *invented* for the unnamed bays. The result should feel like a real Houston tunnel floor, not a museum with a sign that says "this is invented."

**Urban Planner.** I want the master plan to have a *legend* that the design team can use without ambiguity. Three tags: `[D]` documented, `[I]` inferred from archetype, `[X]` invented. Plus one rule: the *spatial relationships* between zones (which food court is connected to which corridor, which building lobby is connected to which tunnel segment) are *all* `[D]` because the Cameron map and the named-segment list document them. The *interior* of each zone is `[I]` for named tenants and `[X]` for unnamed bays. The Urban Planner is happy if the spatial skeleton is `[D]` and the interior fit-out is `[I]` / `[X]`.

**Office Worker.** I will tell you what the real interiors look like. Chick-fil-A at Pennzoil has a counter, a menu board, a queue, and a small dining perimeter. Blackwater Coffee at 1001 Fannin has a counter, an espresso machine, a pastry case, and a few stools. Sparkle Dry Cleaners at McKinney has a counter, a clothing rack, and a "Drop-off / Pick-up" sign. The dry cleaner doesn't need to be invented; the *archetype* is the same in every downtown in America. The point is: the interiors are *generic commercial interiors*, not bespoke architectural designs. A tenant's business is documented; a tenant's specific fit-out is not. The build should use the *generic* fit-out for the named tenants and the *generic* fit-out for the unnamed bays, and the player should not be able to tell the difference.

**Tension points.**
- The Realist wants a strict `[D]` / `[I]` / `[X]` legend. The Gameplay Advocate wants the player not to be able to tell the difference. The Urban Planner wants the spatial skeleton to be `[D]` and the interiors to be `[I]` / `[X]`. The Office Worker wants the interiors to be *generic commercial*, not bespoke.
- The deeper question: when is an invention an *invention* vs. an *inference*? The Realist's answer: a named tenant's fit-out is `[I]` (industry archetype); an unnamed bay is `[X]` (designer fills in). The Office Worker's answer: every fit-out is `[I]` because every commercial fit-out in a Houston tunnel follows a documented archetype (counter, menu board, queue, dining perimeter).
- The Cameron 2015 map's role: it is the *layout* truth. The Realist wants it cited; the Urban Planner wants the spatial relationships treated as `[D]`. Both are right.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The three-tag legend holds. The Realist's standard of invention (the Esperson bar) is the right test. The Gameplay Advocate's standard (the player should not be able to tell) is a useful additional constraint: an invention that *fails* the Esperson bar or *fails* the player-not-telling test should be revised. The Office Worker's "generic commercial" framing is the most useful operationalization of `[I]`. The Cameron 2015 map is the `[D]` anchor for layout; the tenant list (downtownhouston.org) is the `[D]` anchor for named tenants; the tenant interiors follow the generic commercial archetype as `[I]`.

**Decision (binding).**

- **Master plan legend (mandatory).** Every build element in the master plan is tagged with one of three labels:
  - `[D]` = Documented in the research report or in the Cameron 2015 map (cite source).
  - `[I]` = Inferred from industry archetype for the tenant type (cite the archetype: "1970s commercial basement fit-out," "Chick-fil-A prototype," "dry cleaner prototype," "fast-casual food court counter").
  - `[X]` = Invented by the designer to fill a gap (describe the rationale).

- **What is fully documented and may be built without legend tags** (the `[D]` layer): the Cameron 2015 map layout (the named segments: Downtown Tunnel Loop, W. Walker, N. Louisiana, N. Travis, Lamar, E. McKinney); the four food court locations (Esperson 808 Travis, Pennzoil 711 Louisiana, 1001 Fannin / Lamar, McKinney 930 Main); the two direct street-level entries (Wells Fargo 1000 Louisiana, McKinney Garage 930 Main); the 20 ft depth, the 6 mi length, the 95+ blocks, the 200,000 daily users, the 1930s dual origin, the 6 a.m.–6 p.m. weekday hours, the privately-owned building-by-building structure, the climate control (~72 °F), the dropped-ceiling / fluorescent / painted-concrete / VCT-floor standard corridor, the 1970s-Hines-era construction, the 2010s–2020s LED refresh, the floodgate engineering feature, the Tropical Storm Allison 2001 event, the Harris County and St. Joseph discontinuous sections, the Theater District after-hours mode, the skywalk network.

**Urban Planner.** Easter eggs must *compose*. A "Wells Fargo direct access" sign is a *sign*; a "Wells Fargo direct access" sign embedded in a *plaza* with a *revolving door* and a *security guard* and a *down escalator* is a *place*. **Office Worker.** I'll tell you what the actual easter eggs are. The 200,000 daily users is *the* number. The Wells Fargo direct entry is the hack that every office worker knows. ### Topic 6 — Above-Ground City Integration

**The question.** The user said "build a city above the Houston tunnel system." The above-ground city is a *real city* (skyscrapers, parking, the way Houston has a parallel life below street level). The Realist will want full Houston replica. The Gameplay Advocate will want minimal. The Urban Planner will think about city design coherence. The Office Worker will want the daily commute feel. **Realist.** Full Houston replica. The named skywalks are documented. The street grid is documented. The build should reproduce as much of the documented city as the 4:1 linear compression allows.

**Urban Planner.** This is the most important question. The Houston tunnel system is not "a tunnel under a generic city." The above-ground city has to read as a city: streets, sidewalks, parking garages, the one-way grid, the uniform cornice line, the skywalks crossing over the streets, the parallel life of the tunnel below. The Urban Planner does not need a literal Houston replica; the Urban Planner needs a *Houston-vocabulary* city. A fictional downtown that uses Houston's real urban-design language (CBD grid, named streets, named skywalk network, parking-garage typology) is fine. A literal Houston replica would be a different project.

**Office Worker.** I look out the office window and I see a parking garage, a one-way street, another office tower, a skybridge crossing over the street, the next parking garage. The above-ground city is *the view out the window* and it has to feel like a downtown. The Office Worker does not need every named building; the Office Worker needs the *texture* of a downtown — the parking garages, the one-way streets, the skywalk network, the *parallel life* (the tunnel below and the skywalk above are both second-floor equivalents; the street is the *third* level). The above-ground city should be dense enough that you cannot see the edge from any one quadrant.

**Tension points.**
- The Realist wants every named building; the Gameplay Advocate wants only the four anchors. The Urban Planner wants the city to read as Houston without being a literal replica. The Office Worker wants the downtown *texture* (parking garages, one-way streets, skywalk network).
- The deeper question: is the above-ground city a *replica* or a *vocabulary*? The Realist says replica. The Urban Planner and Office Worker say vocabulary. The Gameplay Advocate says neither matters.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The Urban Planner and Office Worker are right. The Houston tunnel system is *under* a downtown; the downtown-ness is part of the build. The four anchor buildings (Wells Fargo, JPMorgan Chase, Pennzoil, Esperson) are rendered in compressed scale with the documented architectural signatures (Wells Fargo's plaza, JPMorgan Chase's tower, Pennzoil's mirrored Philip Johnson trapezoids, Esperson's Art Deco). The above-ground city is `[X]` (designer fills in the generic towers) but uses `[D]` Houston urban-design vocabulary (CBD grid, named streets, named skywalk network, parking-garage typology).

### Topic 7 — Combined-Complex Integration

**The question.** The Houston tunnel system sits UNDERNEATH a city in the valley between the mountain range (Cheyenne Mountain, north) and the coastal plain (SubTropolis, south). The city is the same city the public shaft from SubTropolis (and possibly the service tunnel from Cheyenne Mountain) connects to. The Houston tunnel system is the *urban* leg of the trilogy. How should the city above the tunnel be designed? What is the relationship to the SubTropolis public shaft? What is the relationship to the city street level?

**Realist.** The SubTropolis public shaft and the Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel are *project fictions* — they don't exist in the real SubTropolis or the real Cheyenne Mountain. The build must mark them as such, both in-world (with interpretive signage) and in the master plan (with `[X]` tags). The public shaft especially: in the real SubTropolis, workers arrive by car on a private drive; there is no public transit, no pedestrian shaft. The fiction should be a *discrete addition* to the canonical Houston above-ground city, not a re-imagining of it. The Houston tunnel system itself is a real-world feature; the combined-complex integration is the fiction layer.

**Gameplay Advocate.** The whole point of the combined-complex project is that the player walks between the three sites. The Houston tunnel system is the *urban* leg — the player walks from the tunnel system up to the city street, then up the public shaft to SubTropolis, or down the service tunnel to Cheyenne Mountain (if the service tunnel reaches the city). The transitions have to be *player-traversable*, not just lore tunnels. The public shaft should support a player taking a boat, an elevator, or a ladder up to the SubTropolis grid. The above-ground city should be a *navigable layer* with named streets, skywalks, and a transit hub.

**Urban Planner.** The above-ground city is the *connector*. The three sites are: Cheyenne Mountain (military / secure / single-use, north of the valley), SubTropolis (suburban industrial / civilian / commercial, south of the valley), and Houston tunnel system (urban pedestrian / downtown office, in the valley). The city in the valley is the *layer that holds them together*. The public shaft from SubTropolis lands in the city; the service tunnel from Cheyenne Mountain runs under the valley; the Houston tunnel system is a *feature of the city itself*. The Urban Planner wants the city to be a *real layer* — a real CBD with a real street grid, a real skywalk network, and a real tunnel system underneath. The public shaft and the service tunnel are the combined-complex additions, but the city is the *primary* feature of this site, not the combined-complex transit hub.

**Office Worker.** I have never taken a public shaft from downtown Houston to a limestone mine. I have never taken a service tunnel from downtown Houston to a military bunker. The combined-complex integration is a *project fiction* and the Office Worker is fine with it as long as the fiction does not disrupt the daily commute. The Office Worker's daily commute is: lobby → basement corridor → food court → lunch → lobby. The combined-complex transit hub can land somewhere in the city (the Office Worker doesn't care where), and the city above the tunnel can be a *Houston-vocabulary* downtown. The Office Worker's only requirement: the daily commute (lobby → corridor → food court → lobby) must still work, and the transit hub must not be in the way of the food courts.

**Tension points.**
- The Realist wants the public shaft and service tunnel clearly marked as project fictions. The Gameplay Advocate wants them player-traversable. The Urban Planner wants the city to be the primary layer, not the transit hub. The Office Worker wants the daily commute to still work.
- The deeper question: is the combined-complex integration a *discrete addition* (Realist, Office Worker) or a *redesign* of the site (Gameplay Advocate)? The Urban Planner's "city is the primary layer" framing is the most useful.
- The Houston tunnel system's relationship to the combined complex: it is a *feature of the city*, not a part of the service tunnel or the public shaft. The service tunnel runs *under* the city at 150+ ft depth; the Houston tunnels are at 20 ft depth. The two are physically separate.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The Realist and Office Worker are right: the public shaft and service tunnel are project fictions and must be marked as such. The Gameplay Advocate is right that the transitions have to be player-traversable. The Urban Planner is right that the city is the primary layer. The resolution: the Houston above-ground city is a *real* downtown (Houston-vocabulary), the four named buildings anchor the four quadrants, the public shaft from SubTropolis lands at a small "Combined Complex Transit Hub" plaza at the edge of the city (not in the heart of the CBD), and the service tunnel from Cheyenne Mountain runs under the city at 150+ ft depth (separate from the Houston tunnels at 20 ft depth). The combined-complex integration is *discrete* — it does not disrupt the daily commute or the food courts.

**Decision (binding).**

- **The above-ground city is the primary layer of the Houston site.** It is a representative downtown Houston (4:1 linear compression, 4 named anchor buildings, 8–10 generic towers, 2–3 parking garages, the named street grid, the named skywalk network). The Houston tunnel system is a *feature* of the city, at 20 ft depth. The city itself is the primary thing the player sees when they emerge from the tunnel or descend from the skywalk.

- **The SubTropolis public shaft (`[X]` — project fiction):**
  - **Landing point:** at a small "Combined Complex Transit Hub" plaza at the *edge* of the above-ground city (not in the heart of the CBD), in a buffer block outside the four named quadrants.
  - **Plaza design:** a small open plaza with the public-shaft entrance (a glass-and-steel canopy, a stair down, a small "SubTropolis / Combined Complex Transit" sign, a turnstile, a security guard booth — same as the SubTropolis-side terminus per the SubTropolis discussion Topic 6).
  - **In-world signage:** "Combined Complex Transit Hub — SubTropolis Public Access. SubTropolis is a 270-million-year-old limestone mine north of the river; the public shaft connects downtown to the SubTropolis grid. Travel time: ~10 minutes by elevator." The fiction is clearly marked.
  - **Master plan tag:** `[X]` — project fiction, not in documented Houston tunnel history.

- **The Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel (`[X]` — project fiction, if it reaches the city):**
  - **Path:** horizontal, single-lane, running under the valley at 150+ ft depth, *below* the Houston tunnel system (which is at 20 ft depth). The two are physically separate; the service tunnel does not pass through the Houston tunnels.
  - **Houston-side terminus:** a small service sub-basement at the edge of the city, separate from the tunnel system, with a security gate and a "Service Tunnel — Cheyenne Mountain Complex — Authorized Vehicles Only" sign.
  - **Cross-section:** ~6 × 5 blocks (matches the SubTropolis end per the SubTropolis discussion Topic 6).
  - **Master plan tag:** `[X]` — project fiction.

- **Coordination with the combined-complex team:**
  - The SubTropolis public shaft cross-section is ~5 × 5 blocks (per SubTropolis discussion Topic 6). The Houston above-ground city is sized to receive it (one plaza at the edge, ~10 × 10 blocks).
  - The Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel cross-section is ~6 × 5 blocks (per Cheyenne Mountain discussion Topic 6 / Topic 7 equivalent). The Houston terminus matches.
  - The above-ground city uses Houston urban-design vocabulary (`[D]`) but is a fictional downtown (`[X]` for the specific layout). The combined-complex map should reflect this: Houston is the *urban* leg of the trilogy, with the city as the primary layer and the tunnel system as a feature of the city.
  - The Houston tunnel system does *not* connect to the service tunnel or the public shaft. It is a civilian workplace network with its own hours (weekday 6 a.m.–6 p.m.) and its own access rules (80+ building lobby access, 2 direct street-level entries). The combined-complex transit hub is a separate fiction layer that does not share access with the Houston tunnels.

- **The above-ground city's relationship to the city street level:**
  - The Houston tunnel system is a *feature of the city* — the city's above-ground and below-ground pedestrian networks are both visible and both traversable.
  - The street level (one-way grid, parking, sidewalks) is the *primary public space* of the city; the tunnel level is the *private-building basement network*; the skywalk level is the *second-story private-building connector network*.
  - The three levels (street / tunnel / skywalk) are the *parallel pedestrian life* of the Houston CBD. The build canon is: the player can walk the streets, walk the tunnels, or walk the skywalks, and each is a complete pedestrian experience.

## 4. Tensions and Tradeoffs Acknowledged

The panel made real choices, and the choices have costs. The following are the things the build will *not* be, as a consequence of the decisions above:

- **We will not be a literal-scale replica.** At ~1:4 linear compression, the build is roughly 25% of the real system's linear extent and 25% of its block count. A purist who walks the build will not feel they have walked the Houston tunnel system. They will feel they have walked a *representative slice* of it — the four food courts, the two public entries, the standard corridor, the named skywalk — and that is the panel's call. The Realist accepts this in exchange for a buildable, visitable build.

- **We will not render all 95+ blocks.** The research's recommendation of 6–12 blocks is exceeded slightly (24 blocks) to preserve the named-segment taxonomy, but the build is still a fraction of the real system. The "+" in "95+ blocks" is honored in the signage; the player should understand they are seeing a sample, not the whole system.

- **We will not honor the full multi-tenant directory.** The downtownhouston.org directory lists ~100+ tenants. The build picks 10–12 (Chick-fil-A, Potbelly, Salata, Treebeards, Otto's, Maggie Rita's, Blackwater Coffee, Brown Bag Deli, Sparkle Dry Cleaners, Randie's Barbershop, Shipleys, Kolache Factory) and labels the rest "Vacant / For Lease." The Office Worker notes that real Houston tunnels have ~30% vacancy at any given time, so the "Vacant" bays are themselves a documentary detail, not a loss.

- **We will not show the 1970s Hines era as a "vintage" build.** The 1970s / 1990s / 2010s time-staggered visual cross-section is *embedded* in the corridor segments (one quadrant is 1970s bare concrete, another is 1990s refresh, another is 2010s LED), but the build is not a *period piece*. The Realist's preference for a strict-1970s build was set aside in favor of the time-staggered cross-section, which is more honest to what a current visitor sees.

- **The Esperson food court is the only food court in full detail.** Pennzoil, 1001 Fannin, and McKinney are rendered in compressed form — the food court *type* is documented for each, but the specific stall layout is `[X]`. The Esperson bar sets the standard; the other three clear it but do not match it.

- **The Harris County tunnel and St. Joseph skywalks are reduced to a single sign each.** The Realist wanted these as full builds (they are documented, and the holes in the map are part of the truth). The Gameplay Advocate and Office Worker argued that no real Houston tunnel user has walked the Harris County tunnel (it's a separate system for courthouse workers). One sign at the build's edge is the compromise.

- **The above-ground city is a *representative downtown*, not a literal Houston replica.** The four named anchor buildings (Wells Fargo, JPMorgan Chase, Pennzoil, Esperson) are rendered with their documented architectural signatures; the surrounding 8–10 towers are generic-but-realistic downtown office towers. The Realist wanted every named building reproduced; the Urban Planner and Office Worker wanted the city to read as Houston without being a literal replica. The compromise is the four anchors + the generic cornice line + the named street grid + the named skywalk network.

- **The above-ground city is `[X]` (fictional) but uses `[D]` Houston urban-design vocabulary.** The specific layout of the buffer blocks is invented; the street names, the skywalk connections, the parking-garage typology, the cornice line, the one-way grid are all documented. The player should understand they are walking a *Houston-vocabulary* downtown, not the actual Houston CBD.

- **The combined-complex public shaft and service tunnel are *project fictions*.** They are clearly marked as such (in-world signage and `[X]` master-plan tags), but they are not in the documented Houston tunnel history. A purist who knows the real Houston tunnel system will notice the "Combined Complex Transit Hub" plaza and the "Service Tunnel — Cheyenne Mountain — Authorized Vehicles Only" sub-basement, both of which are not in the real downtown. The trade is interoperability with SubTropolis and Cheyenne Mountain — the fictions are the price of the combined-complex project.

- **The 250,000+ daily users stat is rejected.** Some recent sources cite 250,000+; the Office Worker reads this as a "press release" number. The Realist defers to the consensus 200,000 figure. A player-facing sign with 250,000+ would be inconsistent with the Office Worker's daily reality and is therefore out.

- **The 7-mile length is rejected.** AMLI's "7 mi" is the lone outlier against a strong "6 mi" consensus (Wikipedia, downtownhouston.org, Houston Chronicle, City Cast, Casiola, Secret Houston, HTXTrip, 365things). The Realist insists on the consensus figure; the Gameplay Advocate notes that no player will notice the difference but agrees to keep the canonical 6 mi for honesty.

- **The Tropical Storm Allison detail is a single marker, not a flooded corridor scene.** The Realist's *citation* instinct pulled toward rendering a flooded section; the Office Worker's *feel* instinct pulled toward the daily routine. One small "Tropical Storm Allison — June 2001" marker at the floodgate is the compromise. The build does not depict the disaster; it depicts the engineering response to the disaster (the floodgate).

## 5. Open Questions for the User

The panel has decided what it can decide. The following are the questions the panel flagged for the human / architect that the deliberation could not resolve:

1. **The four named anchor buildings' compression ratio.** The 4:1 linear compression applies to building footprint, but the *vertical* of the named towers is the question. The Realist JPMorgan Chase Tower is 75 stories (compressed to ~18–20 at 4:1). The Office Worker views these towers as the daily view out the window. The architect should confirm the *height* of each named tower (Wells Fargo, JPMorgan Chase, Pennzoil, Esperson) and the height range of the generic downtown towers. The panel did not specify exact heights because the named-tower heights are not all in the public record (Pennzoil is 36 stories, Esperson is two ~18-story Art Deco towers, Wells Fargo is 71 stories, JPMorgan Chase is 75 stories — but the design team should confirm).

2. **The "1930s dual origin" plaque design.** The panel approved one combined plaque at the Wells Fargo entry: "Houston Tunnel System — Established 1930s. First tunnel: Ross Sterling under Fannin (1931) and Will Horwitz under Capitol (1935). Inspired by the Rockefeller Center underground concourse." The architect should decide whether this is one plaque or two (Sterling + Horwitz separately), the size, the material, and the placement. The panel did not specify the design.

3. **The "Combined Complex Transit Hub" plaza design.** The SubTropolis public shaft lands at a plaza at the edge of the above-ground city. The panel approved the *concept* and the *placement* (edge of city, not heart of CBD). The architect should decide the size, the design (open plaza, glass canopy, security guard booth, turnstile, sign), and the relationship to the surrounding buffer blocks. The panel did not specify the design.

4. **The "after-hours shutdown" scene design.** The Tier 3 flavor feature (k) is an after-hours shutdown in one quadrant: lights off, security-locked elevator bank, "Tunnel Closed 6:00 PM" sign. The architect should decide whether this is a *static* scene (always rendered as after-hours) or a *time-of-day* lighting cycle (one quadrant goes dark at 6 p.m. Minecraft time). The Office Worker prefers the time-of-day cycle; the panel did not specify.

5. **The Theater District "performance-night" stub corridor.** The Tier 2 signature feature (j) is a stub corridor with a "Tonight: Performance 7:30 PM" sign and a darkened section beyond. The architect should decide the size, the location, the sign design, and whether the darkened section is *blocked* (a wall with a sign) or *extending* (a real corridor that the player can walk into, eventually looping back or ending in a "Tunnel Closed" door). The panel did not specify.

6. **The "1950s-era minimal corridor segment" design.** The Tier 2 signature feature (i) is a 1950s-era bare-concrete / older-fluorescent / no-drop-ceiling corridor segment representing the 1930s/1950s origin sections. The architect should decide the size, the location, and the level of detail (bare concrete walls, exposed pipes, single fluorescent tube, no dropped ceiling). The Office Worker notes that the Ross Sterling tunnel under Fannin and the Horwitz tunnel under Capitol would have looked like this; the architect should match the visual. The panel did not specify.

7. **Inter-site coordination with the SubTropolis and Cheyenne Mountain teams.** The above-ground city is sized to receive the SubTropolis public shaft (~5 × 5 blocks cross-section) and the Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel (~6 × 5 blocks cross-section). The Houston terminus of each is a `[X]` fiction; the SubTropolis and Cheyenne Mountain termini are specified in their respective discussion documents. The combined-complex team should confirm the cross-sections and the plaza design at the next coordination meeting.

8. **The "T-marker" entrance sign design.** The T-marker is the small "T" sign on the sidewalk that marks a tunnel entry. The research notes it is small and rarely photographed, and the visual-assets catalog flags it as a gap. The architect should design the T-marker based on the Houston convention (small, blue, "T" inside a circle, on a 6-foot pole on the sidewalk at the two public entries). The panel did not specify the design.

9. **The "Vacant / For Lease" bay count and placement.** The research notes ~30% of the food-court bays are vacant at any given time in the real system. The architect should decide how many "Vacant" bays to render in each food court and their placement. The Office Worker notes that vacant bays are a documentary detail, not a loss. The panel did not specify.

10. **The 10–12 named tenant selection.** The panel approved 10–12 named tenants from the documented downtownhouston.org directory. The architect should select the final 10–12 from the documented list, ensuring a mix of food (Chick-fil-A, Potbelly, Salata, Treebeards, Otto's, Maggie Rita's, Blackwater Coffee, Brown Bag Deli, Shipleys, Kolache Factory), services (Sparkle Dry Cleaners, Randie's Barbershop), and a coffee / snack / dessert category. The panel did not specify the final selection.

These ten questions are flagged for the architect and the human project lead. The panel did not block on them; the decisions above are binding even before these are resolved.

## 6. Combined-Complex Coordination Notes

The following are the inter-site decisions the combined-complex team (Cheyenne Mountain, SubTropolis, Houston tunnel system) will need to honor. These are restated from the Topic 6 / Topic 7 decisions and the relevant D-rows above, for ease of reference.

- **Houston is the *urban* leg of the trilogy.** Cheyenne Mountain is military / secure / single-use (north of the valley). SubTropolis is suburban industrial / civilian / commercial (south of the valley). Houston is urban pedestrian / downtown office (in the valley). The combined-complex map should reflect this distinction.

- **The above-ground city is the primary layer of the Houston site.** The city is a representative downtown Houston (4:1 linear compression, 4 named anchor buildings, 8–10 generic towers, 2–3 parking garages, the named street grid, the named skywalk network). The Houston tunnel system is a *feature* of the city, at 20 ft depth. The city itself is the primary thing the player sees when they emerge from the tunnel or descend from the skywalk.

- **The SubTropolis public shaft lands at a "Combined Complex Transit Hub" plaza at the *edge* of the above-ground city.** The plaza is `[X]` (project fiction), with a glass-and-steel canopy, a stair down, a turnstile, a security guard booth, and a "SubTropolis / Combined Complex Transit" sign. The plaza is in a buffer block, not in the heart of the CBD. Cross-section ~5 × 5 blocks (matches the SubTropolis end per the SubTropolis discussion Topic 6).

- **The Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel runs under the city at 150+ ft depth.** The service tunnel is *physically separate* from the Houston tunnel system (which is at 20 ft depth). The service tunnel does not pass through the Houston tunnels. The Houston-side terminus is a small service sub-basement at the edge of the city, with a security gate and a "Service Tunnel — Cheyenne Mountain Complex — Authorized Vehicles Only" sign. Cross-section ~6 × 5 blocks (matches the Cheyenne Mountain end per the Cheyenne Mountain discussion).

- **Both the SubTropolis public shaft and the Cheyenne Mountain service tunnel are *project fictions*, tagged `[X]` in the Houston master plan and in the combined-complex report.** They are clearly marked as not-in-documented-history at the terminus design (interpretive signage) and in the master plan.

- **The above-ground city uses Houston urban-design vocabulary (`[D]`) but is a fictional downtown (`[X]` for the specific layout).** The street names, the skywalk connections, the parking-garage typology, the cornice line, the one-way grid are all documented. The specific buffer-block layout is invented. The combined-complex team should accept the city as a representative Houston-vocabulary downtown, sized to receive the public-shaft transit hub.

- **The Houston tunnel system does not share access with the combined-complex transit hub.** The Houston tunnels are a civilian workplace network with their own hours (weekday 6 a.m.–6 p.m.) and their own access rules (80+ building lobby access, 2 direct street-level entries). The public-shaft transit hub is a separate fiction layer.

- **The three pedestrian levels of the Houston CBD are the *parallel pedestrian life* of the city:** street level (one-way grid, parking, sidewalks, primary public space), tunnel level (private-building basement network, weekday 6 a.m.–6 p.m.), and skywalk level (second-story private-building connector network). The player can walk all three. This is the Houston-specific truth of the build, and the combined-complex report should reflect it.
