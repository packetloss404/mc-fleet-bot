Section 03 · Overview

Executive Summary

A high-fidelity Minecraft replica of the most iconic Cold War command bunker in the world — 1,450 blocks of pink Pikes Peak granite hollowed to hold a city of fifteen free-standing steel buildings, each sitting on a forest of half-ton coil springs, every detail documented, every number debated.

What this build is

The Cheyenne Mountain Complex (NORAD) is a U.S. Air Force / Space Force installation carved between 1961 and 1966 into the granite core of Cheyenne Mountain, a 9,565-foot peak in the Front Range of the Rocky Mountains southwest of Colorado Springs. It became operational on February 6, 1967 as the Combat Operations Center of NORAD, the binational U.S.–Canada aerospace warning command. For forty years it was the primary command post for North American aerospace defense; since 2006 it has been the Alternate Joint Operations Center, with day-to-day operations at nearby Peterson SFB.

The build is a playable, 20–30 minute walk-through of the complex at compressed scale (2:1 vertical, 4:1 horizontal, 2× local upscaling for signature details). The visitor approaches on a switchback road through the forest, enters through a deliberately unassuming concrete arch, walks 800 blocks of curving J-curve tunnel, passes two 25-ton blast doors in a side-branch airlock, and arrives in the main chamber — a 45 × 25 × 18 block vaulted cavern under 300 blocks of pink granite, holding 15 free-standing buildings on visible spring isolators. The Battle Cab (the Command Center) is the visual climax. The 1980 false-alarm plaque — the real history of the place — is on the main path. A 1.5-million-gallon underground reservoir, the chapel, the Granite Inn bar, and the Stargate Command door are the texture.

Why it matters

Cheyenne Mountain is the most mythologized real bunker on Earth. It is the only U.S. Department of Defense underground facility certified to survive a high-altitude electromagnetic pulse attack. It is the single place in the popular imagination associated with the phrase "where the President would go if the bombs fell" — a phrase that, technically, is not quite right (its job is to ensure the retaliation survives, not the government). It is the set of WarGames (1983), the setting of Stargate SG-1 (1997–2007), the inspiration for the 2025 MrBeast "$1 vs $1,000,000,000" video, and the real place where, on June 3, 1980, a 46-cent computer chip nearly started a nuclear war. The build honors the mythology and corrects it.

Section 04 · Research

History & Research

A 1961–1966 engineering project in response to a vulnerability. The threat was a 30-megaton Soviet warhead at 1.2 miles. The response was to hollow a mountain and float a city on springs.

Cold War origins (1956–1961)

On January 15, 1956, General Earle E. Partridge, commander of the U.S. Continental Air Defense Command, directed his staff to plan a hardened underground Combat Operations Center. His existing headquarters at Ent Air Force Base was a small above-ground brick campus that a single Soviet warhead could erase. The threat moved from theoretical to operational on October 4, 1957 when Sputnik 1 reached orbit, demonstrating that Moscow had the rocket technology to put a warhead on any target it chose. NORAD was formalized on September 12, 1958 as a binational U.S.–Canada command; finding it a survivable home was the immediate priority.

Two candidate sites in the Colorado Springs area were evaluated: Blodgett Peak and Cheyenne Mountain. Cheyenne Mountain was selected because its granite is denser and contains fewer soft veins, reducing the chance of catastrophic fracture under blast loading. Other reasons: distance from both coasts (forcing an attacking ICBM to fly a longer polar arc), seismic stability of the Front Range, and proximity to Ent AFB and the existing command infrastructure.

Construction (1961–1966)

  • May 2, 1961 — Utah Construction & Mining Company wins the excavation contract.
  • May 18, 1961 — Excavation begins. Estimated cost: $66 million.
  • 1961–1963 — The mountain is hollowed out: 693,000 tons of Pikes Peak granite removed — the entire equivalent weight of the Pentagon. Smooth-wall blasting advised by Clifton W. Livingston of the Colorado School of Mines kept the cavern walls structurally intact.
  • August 1962 — A geological fault is discovered running through the ceiling of a tunnel intersection. A 1,500-cubic-yard concrete dome is poured and rock-bolted in place at a cost of $2.7 million.
  • 1963 — Continental Consolidated Corporation assembles the interior: 15 free-standing steel buildings on the spring array.
  • May 1, 1964 — Excavation complete.
  • April 20, 1966 — General Dean C. Strother, NORAD commander, formally declares the complex operational as the Combat Operations Center.
  • February 6, 1967 — All planned systems become fully operational. The Space Defense Center, 1st Aerospace Control Squadron, and National Civil Defense Warning Center all move in from Ent AFB.

Final official cost: $142.4 million in 1966 dollars (≈ $1.4 billion in 2024 dollars).

The mountain itself

Cheyenne Mountain consists almost entirely of Pikes Peak Granite, a coarse-grained, pink-to-brick-red syenogranite. It is part of the Pikes Peak batholith, a 1.08-billion-year-old Late-Precambrian pluton. The mineralogy is microcline (pink potassium feldspar), quartz (smoky and milky), plagioclase feldspar, and minor biotite mica. Where exposed, the stone is unmistakable — pink, pale orange, or brick red. It is the same stone used in the Air Force Academy's iconic chapel 12 miles north.

The mountain provides three forms of protection in one package: blast overpressure attenuation (1,800–2,000 ft of solid granite), EMP shielding (the granite attenuates electromagnetic pulse; the buildings inside are also Faraday cages), and thermal and fallout shielding (the same rock mass blocks thermal flash and radioactive fallout). The mountain is not designed to be hit directly. The NORAD public fact sheet states it is "partially resistant" to nuclear attack. It was sized to survive a 30-megaton detonation at 1.2 miles — a near-miss — and to be a low-priority target that attackers would not waste warheads on.

Why this mountain The planners rejected Blodgett Peak (too many soft veins) and chose Cheyenne Mountain specifically because of its solid, unbroken granite character. The mountain is the entire engineering argument. The complex is under the mountain, not in it — in the way a ship is under the sea.

15 buildings, 1,319 springs, a J-curve tunnel

The complex's defining feature is a counterintuitive engineering solution: the operational buildings are not anchored to the rock. They are free-standing, sitting on a forest of large coil springs, isolated from the surrounding granite so that when the mountain shakes, the buildings don't.

As Steven Rose, the deputy director, put it: "The building would bounce, but never touch the granite. We would not lose operations."

  • 15 buildings — 12 three-story and 3 two-story. ~180,000 sq ft of building floor space, ~4.5 acres of building footprint.
  • 1,319 springs — half-ton coil isolators, each ~4 ft long × 20 in diameter, ~1,000 lb each, ~65,000 lb compressive capacity, installed to within 1/16 in of true level.
  • 18 inches of clearance between each building and the surrounding granite. The buildings sway up to 1 inch in normal operation, up to 12 inches in an extreme event.
  • 25-ton blast doors — 20 ft tall, 3 ft thick, hinged outward so blast pressure pushes them closed and tighter. Two of them, on a side-branch of a curved access tunnel, with a positive-pressure airlock between them.
  • The J-curve tunnel — the 2-mile curved access tunnel. The original design was straight; the planners discovered a straight tunnel would have intersected gold mines at Divide, Colorado. The curve was kept for both practical and tactical reasons — a blast overpressure from a surface detonation follows the tunnel, and a straight tunnel would deliver the full force to the blast doors. The curve dissipates ~80% of the force against the granite walls.

The current mission (2006 → present)

Since 2008, NORAD and USNORTHCOM day-to-day operations have been at Peterson AFB, 12 miles northeast. Cheyenne Mountain is the Alternate Joint Operations Center — fully staffed around the clock (~350 weekday, ~120–130 weekend), can assume full control "within minutes" of an order. The current mission: missile warning, space surveillance, aerospace control. The complex is operated by Space Base Delta 1 (USSF) and hosts more than a dozen government and DoD agencies.

The 2015 Raytheon contract — $700 million over 10 years — moved additional systems into the complex, citing EMP resilience as the primary reason. As hypersonic threats have grown, the strategic value of a hardened, cyber-isolated command node has been re-emphasized.

The 1980 false alarm — six minutes from nuclear war

June 3, 1980 A 46-cent computer chip failed in the missile warning network. NORAD reported 200 SLBMs inbound, which revised to 2,200 ICBMs within minutes. National Security Advisor Zbigniew Brzezinski was woken at 3 a.m. He did not wake his wife, calculating she had minutes to live. Bomber crews took their aircraft. The alert was resolved when a third call reported no radar or satellite confirmation. An Office of Technology Assessment scientist later said: "Going to war. And it came damn close to taking the country with it." A nearly-identical false alarm had occurred on November 9, 1979, when a 427M test program was inadvertently uploaded to the live system; 1,400 Soviet ICBMs were reported. Resolved in 6 minutes.

These two near-catastrophic false alarms are the closest documented instances of accidental nuclear war in U.S. history. They are the real reason Cheyenne Mountain matters — not the bunker, but the thin margin. The 1980 false-alarm plaque is on the main path in the build. It is not an easter egg; it is a memorial.

Section 05 · Soul

The Soul of the Place

You approach on a winding mountain road through ponderosa pine, and for a long time there is nothing — a peak, a ridge, an antenna farm, a chain-link fence, and the faint hum of distant generators carried on the wind. Then the road bends and a small concrete-and-steel arch appears in the pink granite, hardly larger than a barn door, with the words CHEYENNE MOUNTAIN COMPLEX bolted across it in flat black letters.

The journey inward

You step inside. The air changes: cooler, drier, smelling of dust and ozone and floor wax. The tunnel curves and you cannot see where you are going. A few hundred meters in, a 25-ton steel door hangs open on a side branch, hinges outward, and you walk under it as if entering a cathedral made of mathematics. Past a second door — the airlock — the tunnel opens into something that should not exist: a city under a mountain. Fifteen windowless steel buildings, each the size of a small office block, sit on a forest of 1,319 half-ton coil springs under a single rock ceiling, lit by fluorescent tubes that hum. Somewhere a generator ticks. Somewhere a screen glows.

This is the feeling: not the horror of the bomb, but the strange, devotional stillness of the species' most expensive admission that it planned for its own ending — and built, with absurd care, a small room at the bottom of the mountain where someone would still be on watch when the ceiling stopped shaking.

The 1,319 springs — the philosophical thesis

The springs are not decoration. They are the thesis of the place. The buildings do not love you. They are not anchored to the rock. They are not anchored to the walls. They are decoupled from the mountain so that when the mountain shakes, the buildings shrug. Resilience is not rigidity. Resilience is decoupling.

The whole design — the curved tunnel, the side-branch blast doors, the airlock, the 1,800 feet of rock above — is the same argument in different materials: assume the worst, twice. The whole thing says: the mountain is not designed to survive. The mountain is designed to make the attacker spend so many warheads on it that they have none left for the things that matter. The mountain is a tax on the enemy's warhead budget.

"The building would bounce, but never touch the granite. We would not lose operations." — Steven Rose, Deputy Director, Cheyenne Mountain Complex · 2015

The three most iconic features

Everything else in the build is a footnote to these three.

1 · The 15 spring-mounted buildings The Money Shot. The only one that would appear on a documentary's title card. The visitor should be able to walk between the buildings and see the springs underneath. This is the engineering made visible — and the engineering is the meaning.
2 · The 25-ton blast doors The Cinematic Reveal. Twenty feet tall, three feet thick, hinged outward. The most photographed feature in the complex. A threat model made visible. The visitor should feel, briefly, in the way one feels small in a cathedral nave.
3 · The J-curve tunnel The Approach. The 800-block gently curving tunnel. The visitor cannot see the far end. The walls change character as you walk. By the time you reach the chamber, you have been inside the mountain long enough to forget the sun.

The real Battle Cab is cozy 1980s console, not theater

Hollywood gave the world a tiered war room with a giant red WOPR computer. The real Battle Cab is a U-shaped array of CRT consoles, beige executive chairs, and time-zone clocks labeled ZULU / HAWAII / PACIFIC / MOUNTAIN / CENTRAL / EASTERN / MOSCOW. It is so 1983 that it is now retro-cool. It is not a theater. It is a 12-hour-shift office, and the absence of stimulation is part of the design.

The build stays closer to the real visual vocabulary — bare rock, fluorescent grey, institutional corridor — and the visitor who has seen WarGames should feel the subtle difference: the actual one is quieter, greyer, more institutional, less cinematic. That dissonance is the cultural hook.

The "backup, not primary" cultural gap

Three things the public believes that are not quite true:

  • "It's the primary command center." It hasn't been since 2006. Day-to-day NORAD operations are at Peterson AFB. Cheyenne Mountain is the alternate — a hardened, cyber-isolated fallback that can assume full control "within minutes."
  • "It would survive a direct hit." It would not. It is rated to survive a 30-megaton detonation at 1.2 miles — a near-miss. A direct hit from a modern precision warhead would defeat it.
  • "Someone is sitting there 24/7 ready to push the button." Closer to true, but more nuanced. The complex is staffed around the clock. Its job today is missile warning, space surveillance, and aerospace control — watching for launches, tracking objects in orbit, coordinating with the sensor network.

The moral hook: the 1980 46-cent chip

The public mythologizes the bunker. The real reason Cheyenne Mountain matters is the thin margin. The 1979 and 1980 false alarms almost started a nuclear war. A 46-cent computer chip and a test tape uploaded by mistake put the United States six minutes from retaliating against a Soviet first strike that did not exist. The system was, in the words of an Office of Technology Assessment scientist, "going to war. And it came damn close to taking the country with it."

This is documented, declassified, and not in the popular imagination. The build must honor it. The 1980 plaque is on the main path. It is not an easter egg; it is a memorial.

Visitor experience — stage by stage

  1. Stage 1 — The drive up. A winding forest road through ponderosa pine. The mountain looms. The sky is big. There is no obvious sign of a military installation.
  2. Stage 2 — The exterior. A small concrete-and-steel arch in the cliff face. The first surprise: the entrance is so small, so mundane. The visitor expected something dramatic; what they got was a maintenance entrance to a state park.
  3. Stage 3 — The J-curve tunnel. A long, slightly curved tunnel carved from pink granite. Rock bolts in the ceiling. Pipes along the wall. The visitor cannot see the far end. The second surprise: the tunnel goes on for a long, long time. You are inside the mountain now.
  4. Stage 4 — The blast doors. Two massive iron doors, hinged outward, with a hand-crank. Awe and a faint, irrational threat. The doors are defense against something.
  5. Stage 5 — The main chamber (the reveal). The tunnel opens into a vast space. Fifteen free-standing steel buildings on a forest of springs under a single rock ceiling. The third and biggest surprise: there is a city under the mountain.
  6. Stage 6 — The Battle Cab. The U-shape of consoles. The wall of displays. The 1980 plaque. The clocks. The climax. The place that was actually on watch when the ceiling stopped shaking.

The Deliberation

Before the build team put down a single block, four voices had to argue it out. The Realist, the Gameplay Advocate, the Architect, the Veteran. This is the record of how they compromised — and what they decided.

The four voices

The Realist
The historian's advocate

If the public record does not show it, do not invent it. Do not import Hollywood into a place where people actually worked.

The Gameplay Advocate
The player-in-the-world advocate

A build that takes 30 real minutes to traverse is a build that nobody experiences. Accuracy without playability is a museum, not a place.

The Architect
The design-coherence advocate

A build that lists every documented feature without a hierarchy will read as a museum. The iconic must dominate; the supporting cast must support.

The Veteran
The feel-of-the-place advocate

The dead fluorescent light, the smell of floor wax and ozone, the way a building is not on the floor but on top of a thousand half-ton springs — these are what distinguish a replica from a memory.

Topic 1 — Scale compression (the contentious one)

At 1:1, the build requires a 2,915-block-tall mountain and a 3,200-block-long tunnel. Unbuildable in vanilla (build height 384). The research suggested 2:1 vertical, 2:1–4:1 horizontal. The argument:

The Realist — "The whole point is the mass of the mountain. If we compress the vertical too aggressively, the visitor walks out of the tunnel and looks up at a foothill and the silent drama of the place is gone."

The Gameplay Advocate — "A 2,900-block-tall mountain in a 1.6 GB Java world is going to chug. The tunnel is the bigger problem — at 1:1, the player walks for ten real minutes down a curving grey tube before the first blast door. Most of them will quit."

The Architect — "What matters is not absolute distance — it is relative proportion. The mountain has to feel heavy above the chambers. The tunnel has to feel like a journey, not a hallway."

The Veteran — "The first thing you notice is that the road curves and you cannot see where you are going. The second is the cold air on your face. The third is the sound — the bus engine, then silence when the door opens. None of that is about a 2-mile number."

Decision Vertical 2:1 (mountain is 1,450 blocks tall — modded build height ≥ 1,024 is mandatory). Horizontal 4:1 (tunnel ~800 blocks, chamber ~45 × 25). Signature detail 2× local for springs, blast doors, and the Battle Cab wall — an authorized exception to uniform compression so they remain visible.

Topic 2 — Must-haves vs. cuts

The Realist wanted everything: the mountain, the portal, the curved tunnel, the two blast doors, the 15 spring-mounted buildings, the chamber array, the four water reservoirs, the six diesel generators, the Granite Inn, the chapel, the medical clinic, the Battle Cab, and the 5 operations centers. "If you cut any of those, the build is no longer a replica — it is a sketch."

The Gameplay Advocate was blunt: "Cut anything that does not earn its visit. Keep the photo-op moments. Cut the dental clinic. Cut the gym. Cut the convenience store."

The Veteran added: "When you are locked inside a mountain for thirty days, the quality-of-life rooms are what keep people sane. Keep the chapel. Keep the absurd — the Granite Inn, the broom-closet Stargate Command door, the Santa tracker banner."

Resolution: include the human spaces as connected side-rooms of the chamber array, not as standalone builds. The 12 must-haves are listed in §3.

Topic 4 — Creative license boundaries

Architecture & Design

1966 institutional grey. 1,400 blocks of compressed granite above. The buildings do not love you. The buildings do not care. The buildings shrug when the mountain shakes.

Design philosophy

This is an industrial cathedral: a 1966 institutional grey hive of 1,319 half-ton coil springs holding fifteen free-standing steel buildings under 1,400 blocks of pink Pikes Peak granite, with a 25-ton blast door standing between you and a 30-megaton sky.

The thesis. The 1,319 springs are not decoration; they are the philosophy of the place. The buildings do not love you. They are not anchored to the rock. They are decoupled from the mountain so that when the mountain shakes, the buildings shrug. Resilience is not rigidity. Resilience is decoupling.

Vintage, not modern. The visual reference is the 1966 institutional Air Force: brushed steel, painted concrete, fluorescent tubes, beige executive chairs, CRT consoles glowing green. The build should not modernize the look. The 1960s bones are still showing in the real complex, and that is the meaning.

The 1,319 springs — the engineering signature

This is the thesis of the build. The visitor must understand, at a glance, that the buildings are not on the floor.

  • Per-building count — 1,319 ÷ 15 ≈ 88 springs per building (average). 3-story buildings: 90–100 each. 2-story: 70–80. The Battle Cab: 110–120.

  • Spring visual — iron_block base (1×1) + 2–3 chain blocks stacked vertically + anvil top cap. 4 blocks tall. A 1-block air gap between the cap and the building floor.

Working Plan

The build is large (1,400+ blocks tall, 800+ blocks of tunnel, 45×25 block chamber, 15 buildings, 1,319 springs) and requires careful phasing. The strategy is outside-in, top-down. Seven phases. Each phase is playable on its own.

Build strategy — outside-in, top-down

Three nested passes:

  • Pass 1 — Above-ground. Mountain shell, approach, antenna arrays, the portal, the parking lot. The exterior is visible and testable.
  • Pass 2 — Tunnel. The 800-block J-curve, the side-branch, the airlock. The visitor can walk the tunnel and reach the blast doors before any chamber work begins.
  • Pass 3 — Chamber. Carve the chamber from the ceiling down (carve out of the mountain rock), then populate the buildings, the springs, the interiors, the reservoir last.

Why outside-in, top-down: the build is playable at every phase, not just at the end. A player can walk the road (Pass 1 done), then walk the tunnel (Pass 2 done), then enter the chamber (Pass 3 done). Mirrors the real construction sequence (1961–1964 mountain hollowed, 1963 buildings inserted, 1966 systems activated).

13.1 Recommended bot command patterns

Mass placement (the mountain shell, the tunnel carving, the chamber carving): Use //fill and WorldEdit sphere/cylinder brushes via the bot's chat relay. The bot should:

  1. Establish build origin: //pos1 at (0, 64, 0).
  2. Load the mountain shell schematic: /schematic load cheyenne-mountain-shell then /schematic paste.
  3. Carve the chamber: //pos1 (-22, 1196, 38) and //pos2 (23, 1214, 63), then //fill minecraft:air.
  4. Carve the tunnel: a series of //fill minecraft:air commands along the J-curve path.

Schematic placement (the portal, the blast doors, the building shells, the antennas): Use /schematic load <name> + /schematic paste <x> <y> <z> [-r rotation].

In-place placement (the building interiors, the easter eggs, the Battle Cab wall of displays): Use the bot's //set and //replace commands for block-by-block placement, or use the mc-fleet-bot API to send setblock commands directly.

mc-fleet-bot API endpoints (for the contractor):

  • POST /api/bots — create a new bot
  • GET /api/bots — list bots
  • POST /api/bots/:name/task — queue a task for a bot
  • GET /api/bots/:name/tasks — list tasks
  • POST /api/commands — dispatch a command to a bot
  • GET /api/commands/:id — get command status
  • POST /api/commands/:id/cancel — cancel a command

Specific commands the contractor will use:

  • Mountain shell: //pos1, //pos2, schematic paste.
  • Tunnel carving: //pos1, //pos2, //fill minecraft:air (for each segment of the J-curve).
  • Chamber carving: //pos1 (-22, 1196, 38), //pos2 (23, 1214, 63), //set minecraft:air.
  • Portal arch: /schematic load cheyenne-portal-arch, /schematic paste 0 1100 -500.
  • Blast doors: /schematic load cheyenne-blast-door, /schematic paste -100 1170 -250 -r 0 (Door 1), /schematic paste -100 1170 -250 -r 180 (Door 2, mirrored).
