# Combined Complex — Discussion Notes (Rework, no-ravine design)

**Project:** mc-fleet-bot Minecraft architecture masterplan
**Document:** 04-combined-complex / 02-design / discussion-notes.md
**Moderator role:** Discussion Moderator (deliberation, not design)
**Date:** 2026
**Status:** Design deliberation. Binding themes are retained only where consistent with the reconciled normalized registry.

> **Authority notice.** Read [../AUTHORITY.md](../AUTHORITY.md) before using this document. This deliberation contains a superseded local `Y=400` contact decision. The controlling Masterplan 04 root registry and contractor brief fix local `Y=200`; Masterplan 05 controls current-world placement. No world edits are authorized.

> **Why this is a rework.** The previous deliberation assumed a V-shaped ravine splitting the mountain range into a granite north face and a limestone south face, with the composite terrane plaque at the ravine bottom. The user has dropped the ravine. The new design is **one continuous mountain range** with a **horizontal granite-limestone contact at a certain elevation** — granite pluton pushed up through overlying limestone, contact exposed in the mountain's flanks and inside the service tunnel. This is real geology (laccoliths, batholith roofs, contact aureoles), not the thrust-faulted composite terrane of the previous design. The composite terrane plaque moves **inside the service tunnel at the contact crossing**, where the walls transition from limestone to granite — geologically honest because that is where the contact actually is.
>
> **What is preserved from the previous deliberation:** the 1 block = 1 m global scale, the 7×7 public shaft with mid-level observation landing at Y = -50, the 6×6 service tunnel with minecart rail and 25-ton blast door, the 6 centerpieces, the 9 easter eggs (with the composite terrane plaque moved), the 30–45 minute focused-play target, the 4-layer defense-in-depth (civilian surface → climate-controlled shallow → industrial limestone → military granite), and the 12 must-haves. The Telling Detail (1.08 Ga granite / 270 Ma limestone) is preserved; its location is just more honest.
>
> **What is genuinely new:** the mountain layout, the contact elevation, the service tunnel as a climbing minecart through the contact, the simpler 6-stage journey without ravine traversal, the composite terrane plaque inside the tunnel, the funicular-plus-road return (no skybridge), and the contact-crossing breccia strip inside the tunnel.

---

## 1. The Panel

This deliberation moderates a structured disagreement between four voices on seven topics that the combined complex has to settle before the design team can start placing blocks. The four voices are the **Realist** (what would Helsinki, Switzerland, or Colorado Springs actually do with a horizontal contact instead of a ravine?), the **Gameplay Advocate** (will a Minecraft player actually make this journey?), the **Architect** (does the world read as one unified piece with a horizontal contact?), and the **Geologist** (is the horizontal granite-limestone contact defensible, and is the contact-crossing plaque honest?). Each voice is allowed to win some topics and lose others. The job of this document is not consensus — it is **decisions**, with the tradeoffs named. The three individual site masterplans (Cheyenne, SubTropolis, Houston) are already complete and binding for their own geometry; this deliberation covers the new inter-site binding decisions plus the global-scale decision that affects all of them.

The change from the previous deliberation is more than cosmetic. The previous design forced a narrative fiction ("composite terrane thrust-faulted klippe") to explain granite on one side of a ravine and limestone on the other. The new design is **real geology**: a granite pluton intrudes into limestone country rock, the contact is roughly horizontal, and erosion has exposed the mountain we see. The plaque that used to live at the bottom of a fictional ravine now lives at the actual geological contact — inside the service tunnel, where the walls transition from cream limestone to pink granite. The Geologist is happier; the Realist is happier; the build is more honest.

## 2. Deliberation Transcript

### Topic 1 — Global Scale

**Question:** What is "one block" in this world? The three individual sites have already settled on different local compressions; the combined complex needs one number that makes the public shaft and the service tunnel scale-consistent with both neighbors, and that works with the new horizontal-contact mountain.

**The Realist (Helsinki / Switzerland school).** A single global scale is non-negotiable, and the no-ravine design doesn't change that. The whole point of the precedents is that *the same unit of measurement runs through everything*: in Helsinki, a 5,500-shelter network and a 220 km technical-tunnel grid share one coordinate system; in Switzerland, the Sasso da Pigna galleries, the Gotthard Base Tunnel bores, and the Airolo town grid all use meters. If the world has three different scales, the connections are visibly faked. I want one number, top to bottom, and the horizontal contact is the cleanest possible reason to use it — the contact is at a single elevation, and that elevation only means something if it's in real meters.

**The Gameplay Advocate.** I hear you, and I'm still scared. 1 block = 1 m globally means the 6-mile Houston tunnels become 9,700 blocks long, the 1,800-ft Cheyenne overburden becomes 549 blocks, the whole complex is a 1,500 × 1,500 block footprint. But the new design is *simpler* than the ravine design — the visitor no longer descends 100 blocks of ravine wall, switches back across a gorge, and climbs 100 blocks back up. The new design is: descend 100 blocks of public shaft, walk 200 blocks of SubTropolis, ride 200–300 blocks of inclined service tunnel up through the mountain, walk 800 blocks of J-curve. That's a tighter experience in fewer distinct spaces. I can live with 1 block = 1 m as long as the world is split into *fully built* core samples and *referenced* outer zones, per the 03-masterplan's "commit to a walkable subset" rule. The horizontal contact actually helps me — it gives the build a single, clean Y-coordinate to aim for, instead of a V-shaped notch that varies in depth.

**The Architect.** 1:1 is the only choice that makes the proportions sing, and the horizontal contact is what makes the proportions *legible*. With a ravine, the depth varied — 80 blocks here, 120 blocks there. With a horizontal contact, the contact is at one elevation across the whole mountain. The mountain is 800 blocks tall, the city is 80 blocks tall, the public shaft descends 100 blocks, the service tunnel climbs 200–300 blocks. Those ratios are the visual story, and they only work at 1:1. If I compress the mountain to 400 blocks, the contact ends up at Y = 200 and the granite cap is only 200 blocks thick — the Cheyenne chamber would be exposed at the surface, and the whole defense-in-depth narrative collapses. I want the proportions to be felt from the coastal plain, not measured with a stick.

**The Geologist.** 1 block = 1 m is what real granite plutons and real limestone country rock actually are. A 1,800-ft granite overburden is 549 m, and that's not negotiable — Cheyenne's blast rating depends on it. A 100-ft limestone roof over SubTropolis is 30 m, and that's not negotiable either. A horizontal contact in a mountain of this scale is typically at some elevation between the surface and the pluton roof — at 1:1, that elevation is a real number (200–400 m, say) that I can cite from the geological literature on laccoliths and batholiths. If we compress vertically, the contact stops being a real geological feature and starts being a kid-stacked-blocks line. The horizontal contact needs real proportions to be visible *as a contact*, and the contact is the build's geological signature.

**Tensions.** Gameplay Advocate is uncomfortable with the absolute size. Realist and Geologist want consistency and real proportions. Architect wants the dramatic proportions preserved. The horizontal contact is a new pressure point: it only works if the mountain is at full real-world scale, because the contact is at a specific elevation. The compression question is *where to fully build* rather than *what scale to use*.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The research is unambiguous: each of the three individual sites has already settled on 1 block = 1 m for its primary geometry, and the Cheyenne masterplan has chosen 1,450 blocks tall (which is 1:1 vertical for the mountain itself, accepting the 2:1 compression on the 9,565 ft real peak only because a 2,915-block mountain is unbuildable at a sensible world footprint). The horizontal contact at Y = ~400 is only meaningful at 1:1, because at any other scale the contact is at a different elevation relative to the chamber and the surface. A single global scale is the only way to make the inter-site connections and the geological contact honest. The Gameplay Advocate's concern is real but is solved by the *what to fully build vs. what to reference* decision (research §10.5), not by *changing the scale*.

**Decision 1 — Global Scale.** **1 block = 1 m globally**, with the explicit understanding that the Cheyenne mountain peak sits at ~Y = 800 (2:1 vertical compression on the real 9,565 ft / 2,915 m peak) to keep the world footprint buildable. The world requires a custom build-height of at least 1,024 blocks (this is already a constraint flagged by the 01-masterplan). The horizontal granite-limestone contact is at a single fixed Y across the whole mountain, and that Y is meaningful only at 1:1 scale. The fully-built core samples are: the public shaft, the SubTropolis chamber (200 × 200 blocks per the 02-masterplan), the SubTropolis horizontal portal, the service tunnel (with the contact crossing fully built and lit), the Cheyenne J-curve and chamber, and the Houston tunnel's 24-block sample. The rest is referenced in silhouette and lore.

### Topic 2 — Mountain Layout (no ravine, horizontal contact)

**Question:** The user has dropped the V-shaped ravine. The new design is one continuous mountain range with a horizontal granite-limestone contact at a certain elevation. What's the actual layout? What's the contact elevation? What's the peak height? Where's the forest line? Where's the snow line? How does the city approach the mountain?

**The Realist (Swiss alpine-planning architect).** A continuous mountain with a horizontal contact is a *laccolith* or a *batholith roof*, and the real-world precedents are clean. The Henry Mountains in Utah, the La Sal Mountains, the Abajo Mountains, the Navajo Mountain — these are all laccolithic ranges where a granite-like intrusion pushed up through sedimentary country rock, leaving a horizontal contact between intrusive igneous rock above and sedimentary rock below. Erosion has stripped the sedimentary cover in some places, exposing the contact. The Swiss have analogues: the Bergell granite intrudes into the surrounding Alps, the Aar granite is exposed in the central Swiss Alps with a roughly horizontal contact against the surrounding limestone and schist. The mountain doesn't need a ravine to be interesting — it needs a *clear contact* and a *clear visual hierarchy* between the two rocks. The asymmetry of the previous design (granite peak taller than limestone hillside) goes away — in a laccolith, the granite IS the mountain and the limestone is the lower flanks. One continuous mountain, with one contact, and a clear approach from the valley.

**The Gameplay Advocate.** I was worried the ravine was the dramatic moment, but actually the no-ravine design gives me something better: a *single, readable* geography. The player walks out of the city, looks up, and sees one mountain. The contact is visible as a horizontal line on the flanks — pink-grey granite above, cream limestone below. The SubTropolis horizontal portal opens on the limestone flank, with a road switchbacking up from the city. The Cheyenne outer portal is on the granite face higher up. The funicular goes from the outer portal to the summit. The service tunnel goes from inside the mountain (SubTropolis) up through the contact to the granite (Cheyenne). The player doesn't have to *cross* anything; they just go *up through* the mountain. The contact is the moment, and the contact is a single Y-coordinate the player can aim for. I love this. The mountain should be tall enough that the contact is visible from the city but not so tall that the build footprint explodes. Y = 800 peak, Y = 400 contact — the contact is exactly halfway up the visible mountain, which is the most readable proportion.

**The Architect.** The silhouette I want is **one continuous mountain, asymmetric in profile, with a single dominant peak.** The peak is granite, sharp, Pikes Peak-style. The flanks are limestone on the lower third, transitioning to granite at the contact. The transition is visible as a horizontal line on the mountain's face — the *geological contact is the architectural line of the mountain*. Below the contact: cream limestone with horizontal bedding planes, oak and maple forest, the SubTropolis horizontal portal at maybe Y = 50 (so vehicles can drive in on a slight grade from the city). Above the contact: pink-grey granite, conifer forest, exposed outcrops near the summit, the Cheyenne outer portal at maybe Y = 200 (so the funicular has a 600-block climb to the summit at Y = 800). The mountain should read as a single piece of geological engineering, not a noise pattern. The contact is the *one line* on the mountain that has to be visible from the city. The city is in a wide flat valley at the base — the previous ravine design had a narrow gorge, but the new design has a *valley*. The mountain rises from the valley floor at maybe Y = 0 (city street grade) to Y = 800 (peak). The mountain is ~600 blocks across at the base in plan, narrowing to a peak.

**The Geologist.** The horizontal contact is the geological *thesis* of this build, and it's real geology — I want to make sure the design team understands what they're building. A horizontal contact in a mountain of this scale is **most plausibly a laccolith or a shallow-level batholith roof**. The granite was intruded as magma at depth, pushed up into the limestone country rock, and either (a) lifted the limestone above it (laccolith) or (b) replaced it in place (batholith roof). Erosion has since stripped some of the overlying limestone, exposing the granite core. The contact is roughly horizontal because the granite was intruded along a horizontal plane of weakness (a bedding plane in the limestone), or because the granite spread laterally under its own weight. In real laccoliths, the contact is *approximately* horizontal but not perfectly flat — it has irregularities, slight dips, and offshoots. For the build, a clean horizontal contact is the most honest representation; the irregularities can be subtle 1-block variations in the contact line, not dramatic. The contact elevation should be high enough to give Cheyenne its 1,800+ ft of granite cover, but low enough that the limestone flanks are visible from the city. **Y = 400 is the right number** — it gives ~400 blocks of granite above the contact (more than enough for the 549-block granite overburden, with the chamber at Y = 250–400), and ~400 blocks of limestone below the contact (room for the SubTropolis chamber at Y = -100 and the horizontal portal at Y = 50). The contact is exposed on the mountain's flanks as a horizontal line — pink granite above, cream limestone below. The contact is also exposed **inside the service tunnel**, which is the most important geological moment of the build.

**Tensions.** All four voices agree on the broad strokes: one continuous mountain, horizontal contact, asymmetric profile, contact at mid-mountain. The real disagreement is the contact elevation. Gameplay says Y = 400 because it's the most readable proportion. Architect says Y = 400 because it gives a clean architectural line. Geologist says Y = 400 because it satisfies both the granite-overburden requirement and the limestone-flanks visibility. Realist is silent on the number. The shared ground is strong: the contact is at a single fixed Y, the mountain is one continuous peak, the city is in a flat valley, the SubTropolis portal is on the limestone flank, the Cheyenne outer portal is on the granite face.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The horizontal contact is real geology (laccolith or batholith roof) and the no-ravine design is *more* geologically honest than the previous ravine design. The contact at Y = 400 is the consensus number: it gives 400 blocks of granite above the contact (the chamber at Y = 250 is 450 blocks below the summit at Y = 800, which is 1,476 ft — slightly less than the real 1,800 ft, but consistent with the 2:1 vertical compression on the peak), and 400 blocks of limestone below the contact (the SubTropolis chamber at Y = -100 is 500 blocks below the contact, which is 1,640 ft — substantially more than the real 100–160 ft, but consistent with the limestone being the *flank* of the mountain rather than the surface rock in a real SubTropolis setting). The flank limestone is the geological compression: in real SubTropolis, the limestone is the surface rock; in the build, the limestone is the lower mountain flank, and SubTropolis is carved into it. The contact is the design.

**Decision 2 — Mountain Layout.**
- **One continuous mountain range**, single dominant peak silhouette (Pikes Peak-style), occupying ~600 × 800 blocks in plan.
- **Mountain peak: Y = 800** (granite, sharp triangle, pinkish-grey syenogranite, exposed outcrops near summit, snow line at Y = 700).
- **Horizontal granite-limestone contact at Y = 400** (mid-mountain). The contact is a single fixed Y across the whole mountain. The contact is exposed in the mountain's flanks as a visible horizontal line and inside the service tunnel as the geological moment of the build.
- **Above the contact (Y = 400 to Y = 800):** granite. Conifer forest (pine, spruce) on the lower slopes, transitioning to exposed outcrops and snow near the summit. The Cheyenne chamber is at Y = 250–400, with 400–550 blocks of granite above it.
- **Below the contact (Y = 0 to Y = 400):** limestone. Grey-cream Bethany Falls limestone, horizontal bedding planes visible, oak and maple forest on the lower slopes. The SubTropolis chamber is at Y = -100, with 100+ blocks of limestone above it. The SubTropolis horizontal portal opens on the limestone flank at Y = 50, with a paved road switchbacking down to the city.
- **City in a wide flat valley at the mountain base** (city street grade at Y = 0). The mountain rises directly from the valley floor. No ravine.
- **Approach from the city:** the player sees the mountain as a single dominant peak with a visible horizontal contact line on its flanks. The SubTropolis portal road switchbacks up the limestone flank; the public shaft entrance is at the city surface (in the city, not on the mountain); the Cheyenne outer portal is on the granite face at Y = 200, with the funicular going up to the summit at Y = 800.
- **Forest and snow lines:** conifer forest on the granite above Y = 400, oak/maple forest on the limestone below Y = 400, snow line at Y = 700 on the granite summit. The vegetation is *geology-aware* — different forests on different rocks, just as in real mountain ranges.

### Topic 3 — Public Shaft Design

**Question:** What cross-section, what mode (mechanical lift, stair, escalator), and what is the journey from city surface to SubTropolis? Largely unchanged from the previous deliberation, but the context is now a continuous mountain rather than a ravine.

**The Realist.** 5×5 is the working number — the SubTropolis master plan specifies it, and the Houston master plan is at 1:1 corridor scale. Real SubTropolis doesn't have a vertical public shaft (it's a horizontal mine), but the closest real-world precedent is Helsinki's 90 dual-use facilities, which are *basements of surface buildings* entered from above — vertical, with a guard booth and a turnstile. The mode should be a **mechanical lift**, not a stair: public infrastructure at this scale (50–100 m vertical) is universally a lift in the real world. A stair is what you do when you can't afford a lift. The SubTropolis-to-city journey is an *employee entrance 95% of the time and an emergency egress 5% of the time*, per the Helsinki model — that is the design language. The no-ravine design doesn't change any of this; the shaft is independent of the mountain's surface geometry.

**The Gameplay Advocate.** 7×7 is the right number: 5×5 inner for the lift, 2-block outer ring for an emergency stair and a service chase. The mode should be a **lift as the primary**, with a **secondary emergency stair** in the outer ring. The 100-block vertical descent is the moment; without a mid-level landing, it's monotonous. The Y = -50 mid-level observation landing breaks the monotony and gives the player a *breath* between two descents. The no-ravine design actually makes the public shaft *more* important — without a ravine to provide drama, the public shaft is the *only* major vertical descent in the build. The shaft has to do more work, and the mid-level landing is the engagement moment that the ravine used to provide.

**The Architect.** The public shaft is one of the build's two architectural centerpieces. The 7×7 with a centered 5×5 lift and a 2-block outer service ring is the right call. The mode is *both* lift and stair — the lift is the mechanical primary, the stair is the visible architectural counterpoint. The mid-level observation landing at Y = -50 is the moment: a 7×7 room with a single glass window looking out at a *G-Cans-style* concrete pillar in the city's underground utility corridor, lit dramatically, visible from the city side. The surface entrance is a small architectural object — a glass-and-steel pavilion with a "SubTropolis — Public Access" sign, the T-marker borrowed from the Houston visual language, a guard booth, a turnstile. The descent is *deliberate*, not accidental. With no ravine, the public shaft is the *only* moment of vertical descent in the build's first half — it has to be the payoff for the city scene.

**The Geologist.** The shaft cuts through the limestone — that part is fine, limestone takes a vertical bore cleanly. What I want to see at the mid-level landing is *a glimpse of the rock*: a single block of exposed limestone on the observation-room wall, labelled with its age (270 Ma, Pennsylvanian), so the player understands the descent is *into real rock* and not a generic basement. That's a 30-second read for the curious player, and it pays off the geological story. The shaft is also a *teaser* for the contact — when the player descends through the shaft, they're descending *into* the limestone layer, and the contact (which is above them at Y = 400) is the destination. The shaft is the geological setup; the service tunnel is the geological payoff.

**Tensions.** Largely the same as the previous deliberation: Gameplay and Architect want 7×7 with the outer ring; Realist says 5×5 matches the master plans; Architect and Gameplay want the mid-level landing as a "breath"; Geologist wants a single block of labelled rock. The new wrinkle is that without a ravine, the public shaft carries more of the dramatic weight of the build. The shared ground: 7×7 is the right number, the mid-level observation landing is non-negotiable, the mode is mechanical lift with a visible emergency stair.

**Moderator's synthesis.** Same as the previous deliberation: the SubTropolis master plan's 5×5 is *the lift core*, not the full shaft. Wrapping it in a 2-block outer ring for service + emergency stair makes the shaft visually richer and gives the architect something to design without changing the lift geometry. The mode is mechanical lift (Realist is right — public infrastructure at this scale is a lift in the real world), with a visible emergency stair in the outer ring. The mid-level observation landing at Y = -50 is the single most important interior moment of the public shaft, and it should be designed as a *place*, not a *function*. Without a ravine, the shaft is the build's primary vertical descent, and the landing is the build's primary interior moment. Geologist's labelled rock is a small detail that pays off the geological story; it costs one block and one sign.

**Decision 3 — Public Shaft Design.**
- **Cross-section: 7×7 blocks total**, with a 5×5 inner lift core and a 2-block outer ring (1-block emergency stair on one side, 1-block service chase on the other).
- **Mode: mechanical lift** as primary, with the emergency stair visible through a glass wall in the outer ring.
- **Length: 100 blocks of vertical descent** (Y = 0 to Y = -100), SubTropolis floor at Y = -100.
- **Mid-level observation landing at Y = -50**: a 7×7 room with a single glass window looking out at a G-Cans-style concrete pillar in the city's utility corridor, plus one labelled block of exposed limestone (270 Ma, Pennsylvanian) on the inner wall.
- **Surface entrance:** a 7×7 glass-and-steel pavilion at the city surface with a guard booth, a turnstile, a "SubTropolis — Public Access" sign, and a T-marker (visual signature borrowed from the Houston visual language).
- **Bottom lobby:** small security gate, turnstile, "Welcome to SubTropolis" sign, opening onto the SubTropolis main avenue (Hushpuckney) with the SubTropolis grid visible beyond.

### Topic 4 — Service Tunnel Design (with horizontal contact crossing)

**Question:** What cross-section, what mode (minecart rail, road, pedestrian), and what is the journey from SubTropolis to Cheyenne? The tunnel now climbs through the horizontal contact instead of crossing under a ravine. The composite terrane plaque is at the contact crossing inside the tunnel.

**The Realist.** 6×6 is the consensus number from the research — it's the SubTropolis 6-block corridor width and the Cheyenne J-curve interior, and at 1:1 it's close to the Zojila Tunnel's 9.5 × 7.57 m horseshoe. The mode is **narrow-gauge minecart rail**, no question. The SubTropolis has 2.1 mi of real rail; the Cheyenne J-curve was originally driven by mining crews with rail-based muck cars in the 1960s. A road tunnel between the two facilities would be wrong tonally — SubTropolis is freight, Cheyenne is military, and the rail is the through-line. The **25-ton blast door at the Cheyenne end** is canonical — it's a real Cheyenne feature (smaller than the J-curve main blast doors but still imposing) and it's the architectural climax of the tunnel. The new design (climbing through the contact instead of crossing under a ravine) is actually *cleaner* — the previous design required the tunnel to dip below the ravine stream and climb the other side, which was a contrived geometry. The new design is just an inclined tunnel going up through the contact, with the contact crossing in the middle of the ride. The tunnel length is naturally longer (200–400 blocks instead of 80–120) because of the vertical climb, but the ride is the same — a 2–4 minute minecart experience.

**The Gameplay Advocate.** Minecart is the only fun option. A road is just walking with extra steps. A pedestrian tunnel is the worst — the player has already been walking. The minecart ride is the *one moment of speed* in the whole build, and it gives the player a sense of *movement* through a featureless rock corridor that walking would not. With the ravine gone, the minecart ride is *the* build's payoff for the SubTropolis visit. The contact crossing is the geological moment — the player sees the walls transition from cream limestone to pink granite as they ride. I want the ride to be 2–4 minutes (200–400 blocks), with the contact crossing in the middle. The tunnel should climb at a steady grade (1:3 to 1:5), with no sudden drops or surprises. The 25-ton blast door at the end is the climax — visible from the approaching minecart as a single block of darker color, then a 2-block-tall door, then the checkpoint corridor beyond.

**The Architect.** The 6×6 cross-section needs to be *split* — 4 blocks for the rail, 2 blocks for a utility strip (water pipe from Cheyenne's reservoirs as backup for SubTropolis's fire protection, a power line, a fiber-optic line, an emergency-escape corridor). The 25-ton blast door at the Cheyenne end is the architectural climax of the service tunnel. I want the door to be visible from inside the minecart as the cart approaches — a single block of darker color, then a 2-block-tall door, then the checkpoint corridor beyond. The SubTropolis end is open, with a "U.S. Space Force — Authorized Personnel Only" sign and a 1-block security gate, but no blast door — the contrast (open industrial → 25-ton military) is the story. The new design has a *gradient* instead of a transition: the walls of the service tunnel start as cream limestone at the SubTropolis end, transition through a 20-block contact zone (with breccia visible in the walls), and end as pink granite at the Cheyenne end. The gradient is the design language of the tunnel, and the contact crossing is the gradient's midpoint.

**The Geologist.** The composite terrane plaque is now at the **contact crossing inside the service tunnel** — this is the most important geological moment of the build, and it's the most honest location for the plaque. In the previous ravine design, the plaque was at the bottom of a fictional ravine; in the new design, the plaque is at the actual contact between granite and limestone, visible in the tunnel walls, in the breccia zone, in the rock itself. The plaque should be **a 1-block carved stone in a small alcove at the contact crossing**, lit by a single redstone lamp, surrounded by 1–2 blocks of thrust-fault/contact breccia (mixed granite-and-limestone rubble). The plaque text should explain what the contact is and why the two rocks are in one mountain: "Granite-Limestone Contact — Pikes Peak granite (1.08 Ga) intruded into Bethany Falls limestone (270 Ma). The contact is exposed in the tunnel walls." I want the breccia zone to be a real geological feature — not a fantasy, but a 10–20 block section of tunnel where the walls are visibly mixed, with chunks of granite in a limestone matrix (or vice versa). The breccia is the *evidence* of the contact.

**Tensions.** All four agree on 6×6 and minecart. Gameplay and Architect want the tunnel to *feel* the climb (steady grade, visible contact crossing); Geologist wants a substantial breccia zone at the contact crossing; Realist wants the 25-ton blast door to be the climax. The shared ground is strong; the disagreements are about *how elaborate* the contact crossing is (a 1-block plaque, a 10-block breccia zone, or a 20-block breccia zone).

**Moderator's synthesis.** The research recommendation is unanimous on 6×6 and minecart. The 4-block rail + 2-block utility split is the architecturally right call and is consistent with the Zojila Tunnel's horseshoe profile. The 25-ton blast door is non-negotiable — it's a real Cheyenne feature, it's the visual climax, and the Gameplay Advocate agrees it should be visible. The new design (climbing through the contact) is cleaner than the previous design (crossing under a ravine) — the geometry is naturally an inclined tunnel, the contact crossing is in the middle, and the breccia zone is the geological moment. Geologist's breccia zone is the right call: a 10–20 block section of tunnel with mixed rock walls, with the plaque in a small alcove at the midpoint. The breccia is what you'd actually see at a contact between granite and limestone — it's the rock evidence of the intrusion. The composite terrane plaque is the build's most important single-block detail.

**Decision 4 — Service Tunnel Design.**
- **Cross-section: 6×6 blocks total**, with a 4-block inner rail corridor and a 2-block utility strip (1-block water/power line, 1-block emergency-escape corridor).
- **Mode: minecart rail** (1-block gauge, single track).
- **Length: 200–400 blocks**, depending on the chosen route. The tunnel starts at the SubTropolis chamber (Y = -100) and climbs to the Cheyenne outer portal (Y = 200), a 300-block vertical climb. At a grade of 1:3, the tunnel is ~900 blocks long; at a grade of 1:5, ~1,500 blocks. **The design team should target 300–500 blocks total length** with a steady grade, optimizing for a 2–3 minute minecart ride.
- **Alignment: straight inclined tunnel** climbing at a steady grade from SubTropolis to Cheyenne. No dips, no sudden climbs. The contact crossing is at the midpoint of the tunnel.
- **SubTropolis end: open**, with a 1-block security gate, a turnstile, a "U.S. Space Force — Authorized Personnel Only" sign, and access from the SubTropolis main avenue.
- **Cheyenne end: 25-ton blast door** at the outer portal, with a 4-block concrete-and-granite checkpoint corridor, a guard booth, and the door visible from the approaching minecart. Beyond the door, the J-curve access tunnel (800 blocks per the 01-masterplan) leads to the chamber.
- **Wall gradient:** the walls start as cream limestone at the SubTropolis end, transition through a 10–20 block breccia zone (mixed granite-and-limestone rubble) at the contact crossing, and end as pink granite at the Cheyenne end. The lighting and material palette gradient mirrors the rock gradient.

## 4. Tensions and Tradeoffs Acknowledged

**What we lost by dropping the ravine.** The ravine was a *visual signature* — a V-shaped notch in the mountain, visible from miles away, with a thin line of green at the bottom. The continuous mountain doesn't have that single iconic feature. Instead, the new design's signature is the **horizontal contact line** on the mountain's flank, visible from the city as a clear color transition from cream limestone below to pink granite above. This is more *subtle* than the ravine but more *honest* — real mountain ranges have visible geological contacts, not dramatic gorges. The trade: the build is less immediately recognizable as "the ravine build" and more recognizable as "the horizontal contact build." The design team should make the contact line *visible* from the city — clear color change, possibly a thin strip of exposed breccia along the contact, and a signage marker at the SubTropolis portal road indicating the contact crossing.

**What we gained by dropping the ravine.** The composite terrane plaque is now at the *actual contact* — inside the service tunnel, where the walls transition from limestone to granite. This is the most honest possible location for the plaque. The previous ravine design required a "composite terrane plaque" to explain a *fictional* geology (granite on one side, limestone on the other, requiring a thrust-faulted klippe). The new design requires a "granite-limestone contact plaque" to explain *real* geology (a granite pluton intruded into limestone country rock). The plaque is shorter, more honest, and more scientifically accurate. The Geologist wins; the Realist wins; the build is more honest.

**What we lost by deciding the contact is at Y = 400 (not higher or lower).** A higher contact (Y = 600) would give more limestone flanks and a smaller granite cap; a lower contact (Y = 200) would give a smaller limestone zone and a larger granite cap. Y = 400 is the consensus number — it gives 400 blocks of granite above the contact (more than enough for the 1,800-ft Cheyenne overburden, accepting the 2:1 vertical compression on the peak) and 400 blocks of limestone below the contact (room for the SubTropolis chamber and the horizontal portal). The trade: the contact is exactly halfway up the mountain, which is a *clean* proportion but maybe a *too-clean* one. A real laccolith contact would be at some irregular elevation, not exactly halfway. The design team can introduce 1-block variations in the contact line (slight dips, offshoots) to break the perfect horizontality — this is geologically honest and visually interesting.

**What we lost by deciding on a mechanical lift, not a stair, for the public shaft (Decision 3).** A stair is more visually Minecraft-native (you can see the player descend step by step) and is more *honest* about the depth (a 100-block stair is *obviously* a 100-block stair; a lift is a black box that takes you from top to bottom in 30 seconds). A lift is the right real-world choice but is the less-engaging Minecraft choice. We chose the lift because the public shaft is *civilian infrastructure*, not a Minecraft obstacle, and the mid-level observation landing is the engagement moment. The visible emergency stair in the outer ring is the architectural compromise. **The no-ravine design makes the public shaft more important** (without a ravine, the shaft is the build's primary vertical descent), so the lift's pacing concern is amplified. The design team should make the lift ride as engaging as possible — slow speed, glass walls showing the descent, a single redstone lamp per 10 blocks of shaft for visual rhythm.

**What we lost by deciding on a minecart rail, not a road, for the service tunnel (Decision 4).** A road allows the player to walk or drive through the tunnel at their own pace and to stop and look at features. A minecart commits the player to the rail's pace and doesn't allow stops except at the ends. The minecart is more *dramatic* (the speed through the tunnel is a moment) but less *explorable*. We chose the minecart because the service tunnel is a *through-route*, not a destination; the player is moving from SubTropolis to Cheyenne, not exploring the tunnel itself. The geological moment at the contact crossing is the one pause. **The no-ravine design makes the minecart ride more important** (without a ravine to traverse on foot, the minecart is the build's primary horizontal transit), so the ride has to do more work. The design team should make the contact crossing *visible from the minecart* — the breccia zone is 10–20 blocks, the plaque is in an alcove that the minecart passes slowly, the lighting shifts from limestone-white to granite-pink as the cart climbs.

**What we lost by deciding on a 2-mode return, not a 3-mode return (Decision 5).** The previous ravine design had funicular + skybridge + road (3 modes). The new no-ravine design has funicular + road (2 modes). The skybridge is dropped because there is no ravine to span. The 2-mode return is cleaner but loses the *variety* of three modes. The funicular is the *Swiss moment* (a real alpine funicular to the summit of a granite peak), and the road is the *approach in reverse* (descending through the forest to the city). The design team can add interest to the road return by routing it through both biomes — down the granite side through conifer forest, then across the contact, then down the limestone side through oak/maple forest to the city. The road return becomes a *geological tour* of the mountain's flank, with the contact crossing visible on the way down.

**What we lost by adding the granite summit observation platform as a 7th centerpiece (Decision 6).** The previous design had 6 centerpieces. The new design has 7 (the summit observation is new). The trade: the build has one more architectural object to design, but the summit observation is the *outdoor counterpart* to the indoor plaque — the player sees the contact from inside (in the tunnel) and from outside (at the summit), and the two observations are complementary. The 7th centerpiece is the *narrative resolution* of the journey. The design team should make the summit observation feel like a *summit* — a small architectural object (a 7×7 viewing platform with a low wall, a flag, and the rock identification chart) at the highest point of the build.

**What we did not lose.** The defense-in-depth narrative is intact. The civilian → industrial → military arc is intact. The composite terrane geology is intact (and more honest). The 4-layer layering is intact. The 12 must-haves from the previous deliberation are intact (with the composite terrane plaque moved to a more honest location). The 6+1 centerpieces give the build a clear architectural rhythm. The 9 easter eggs give the build cultural touchstones without overwhelming the build. The Helsinki + Switzerland + Colorado Springs framing is intact in the "Three Sites, One Mountain" sign at the summit. The Telling Detail (1.08 Ga granite / 270 Ma limestone contact) is preserved; its location is just more honest.

## 5. Open Questions for the User (the Architect)

The deliberation resolved the seven topics, but a few questions remain that need a human call before the design team starts:

1. **Contact elevation: Y = 400 exactly, or with intentional variation?** Y = 400 is the consensus number, but a real laccolith contact has slight dips and offshoots. The design team can introduce 1-block variations in the contact line for geological realism, or commit to a perfectly horizontal contact for visual cleanness. The Architect's preference should decide.

2. **Service tunnel grade: 1:3 (900 blocks) or 1:5 (1,500 blocks)?** The 300-block vertical climb from SubTropolis to Cheyenne can be achieved at different grades. A 1:3 grade is steeper but shorter; a 1:5 grade is shallower but longer. The minecart ride time is similar (2–3 minutes either way), but the build cost is different. The Architect's preference should decide, with awareness that 1,500 blocks of service tunnel is a substantial addition to the build.

3. **SubTropolis horizontal portal: at Y = 50 (city-edge) or higher on the limestone flank?** The portal is on the limestone flank of the mountain, with a paved road from the city. A Y = 50 portal is accessible by a short switchback road; a Y = 200 portal requires a longer, more dramatic switchback. The Architect's preference should decide.

4. **Funicular: from the Cheyenne outer portal (Y = 200) to the summit (Y = 800) — 600 blocks of funicular.** This is a substantial funicular. Real funiculars are typically 100–1,000 m vertical. A 600-block funicular is at the high end. The design team should confirm the funicular is in scope and is consistent with the 01-masterplan's overall design language.

5. **Road return: down the granite side, the limestone side, or winding through both?** The road switchbacks from the summit to the city. Down the granite side: conifer forest, dramatic. Down the limestone side: oak/maple forest, more gentle. Winding through both: longer road, more geological interest, but more switchbacks. The Architect's preference should decide.

6. **The 7 architectural centerpieces — are they all in v1, or are any deferred?** The blast door, observation landing, and plaque are non-negotiable. The 3 surface entrances (public shaft pavilion, SubTropolis portal, Cheyenne outer portal) are settled as architectural objects. The granite summit observation is new and adds a 7th centerpiece. The design team should confirm all 7 are in v1 scope.

7. **The 9 easter eggs — all in v1, or are any deferred?** All 9 are passive (signs and blocks, not interactive). They are buildable in the v1 budget, but the design team should confirm. The composite terrane plaque (now at the service tunnel contact crossing) is the most important and must be in v1.

8. **The "Three Sites, One Mountain" sign — at the granite summit, or also at a city-surface location?** The summit is the dramatic location. The city-surface location is more accessible. The Architect's preference should decide.

9. **The 1,500 × 1,500 world footprint — is that the buildable max, or can we go larger?** The research says 1,500 × 1,500 is the recommended footprint. The Minecraft world-generation limits may push this up (a 3,000 × 3,000 world is buildable but is heavier to load). The design team should confirm the world settings.

10. **Render distance and simulation distance for the live build?** The research recommends view-distance 16 and simulation-distance 12. The design team should confirm the player can handle these settings on the target hardware.

11. **What is the visitor's *first view* of the build?** The research says the player arrives via the coastal plain and sees the city with the mountain range behind. Is that the *intended* first view, or should the player spawn closer (inside the city, at the public shaft entrance)? The Architect's preference should decide.

12. **The horizontal contact at Y = 400 — should the contact itself be a *visible* architectural feature (a clear color line on the mountain's flank), or a *subtle* one (a 1-block transition in the rock palette)?** A clear contact is more dramatic; a subtle contact is more geologically realistic (real contacts often have vegetation covering them). The Architect's preference should decide, with awareness that the contact is the build's *geological signature* and should be visible to the curious player.
