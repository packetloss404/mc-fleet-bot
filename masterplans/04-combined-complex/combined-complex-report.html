06 / Real-World Precedents

What other countries have built versions of this

Switzerland — the Sasso da Pigna / Festung San Carlo / AlpTransit model

The Swiss Réduit national (Reduit) was a WWII-era strategy to fall back into the Alps and hold the Alpine passes indefinitely. The Gotthard anchor is the most relevant:

  • Fort Sasso da Pigna (Festung A 8385) sits on the Gotthard Pass, built 1941–1943, 2,400 m of galleries, four 105 mm gun positions, a 500-man garrison, an 87-bed hospital, declassified 2001, opened as the Sasso San Gottardo Museum in 2012.
  • Festung San Carlo (A 8390) is the prototype Réduit artillery fortification, built 1942, declassified 1998–1999, converted 1998–2004 into La Claustra — a four-star "underground monastery" hotel and seminar center with 17 rooms, 25 beds, dining hall, bistro, library, spa, and 6 conference rooms. 4,000 m² at 2,050 m above sea level, 200 m back from a 200 m entrance tunnel bored into the Gotthard massif.
  • Gotthard Base Tunnel, 57.09 km long, opened 1 June 2016, is the world's longest and deepest railway tunnel, with up to 2,300 m of rock cover, costing CHF 12.2 billion over 17 years of construction. It runs under the same Gotthard massif that hosts the Sasso da Pigna and La Claustra.
  • The town of Airolo sits in the valley floor below all of this, at 1,179 m above sea level.

Switzerland is the real-world proof that a single mountain can simultaneously host a hardened military bunker, a converted-to-civilian-use former bunker, a record-setting civilian transport tunnel, and a civilian town in the valley below — and that this can be operated as a coherent tourism and infrastructure cluster. The v2.0 combined complex is the Minecraft version of this.

Finland — the Helsinki Underground Master Plan

Helsinki is thought to be the only city in the world with a written, binding, citywide underground master plan:

  • ~10 million m³ of underground space beneath the city
  • ~500 individual subterranean facilities
  • 220 km of technical tunnels
  • ~5,500 civil defense shelters with space for ~900,000–1,000,000 people — in a city of ~700,000 residents
  • ~90 facilities with dual civilian / civil-defense use
  • An average of 1 m² of underground per 100 m² of ground surface

Helsinki is the model of a civilian city that happens to sit on top of a defense network. The 90 dual-use facilities are the key insight: civilian and military underground functions are not separated; they share infrastructure, with the use case (peace vs. emergency) as a switch. The combined complex's public shaft is a single 5×5 block version of Helsinki's civilian-by-default, defense-by-override design philosophy.

Can a single mountain range host both at a horizontal contact?

The two rocks are different ages, different origins (intrusive igneous vs. marine sedimentary), different tectonic settings, and different positions in a stratigraphic column. In a real mountain range, the basement (granite) is at the bottom; the cover (limestone and other sediments) is on top, if the cover was deposited after the basement crystallized.

The v2.0 design uses a laccolithic geometry — a real geological configuration where a granite pluton intrudes into the overlying limestone country rock from below, lifting the limestone into a dome and creating a roughly horizontal contact between intrusive granite above and sedimentary limestone below. Erosion has since stripped some of the overlying limestone, exposing the granite core as the mountain's peak. Real-world examples: the Henry Mountains (Utah), the La Sal Mountains, the Abajo Mountains, the Navajo Mountain, the Bergell granite (Swiss Alps), the Aar granite (central Swiss Alps).

For the combined complex, the build is a real geological configuration, not a geographic compression. The mountain is a laccolith — a granite pluton that pushed up through limestone, leaving a horizontal contact at Y=200. The contact is visible at the surface (a 1–2 block color-change ring on the south face, full 800-block E–W extent) and inside the service tunnel (a wall-material transition at the contact crossing). The plaque at the contact crossing is at a real geological boundary.

The 7 most pressing (v2.0)

1 — Contact Elevation (NOW BINDING: Y=200)

Y=200 is binding. Confirm the contact elevation is correct.

The contact elevation is Y=200 per the user brief, site-plan.md, and site-coordinates.json. The service tunnel contact crossing, the surface contact ring, the Cheyenne outer portal placement, the SubTropolis horizontal portal's pink contact band — all reference Y=200. Note: design-plan.md (older draft) uses Y=100. Y=200 is the binding call. An off-by-100-blocks error here breaks the entire build.

2 — Service Tunnel Gradient (NOW BINDING: 3.33:1)

3.33:1 grade is binding. Confirm the tunnel geometry.

The service tunnel is an ascending inclined minecart bore, 6×6 × 120 blocks, with a 3.33:1 grade (Y=0 to Y=200, 200 blocks of elevation gain over 60 blocks of horizontal climb, then lateral at Y=200 for the remaining 60 blocks). The 3.33:1 grade requires powered rails every 4 blocks for the minecart to climb. The tunnel does not start at the sub-basement floor (Y=−160); it starts at the SubTropolis chamber's NW corner at Y=0 (chamber ceiling level, the same Y as the city surface). This is a simpler, more honest geometry.

3 — SubTropolis Horizontal Portal Position (NOW BINDING: Y=0)

Y=0 portal position is binding. Confirm the drive-in geometry.

The portal is at (0, 0, −300), on the south face of the mountain (the city-facing side), at city level (Y=0), accessible by a switchback road from the city plaza. Vehicles drive in on a level road (no climb, no descent). The switchback road from the city climbs laterally up the south face of the mountain and arrives at the portal at Y=0. This matches the real SubTropolis drive-in portal model.

4 — Return Route (NOW BINDING: funicular + road, NO skybridge)

Funicular + summit road is binding. NO skybridge.

The return is funicular + summit road. No skybridge. The previous v1.0 design's skybridge (spanning the ravine) is removed because there is no ravine to span. The funicular goes directly from the outer portal (0, 200, −420) to the granite summit (0, 800, −500); the summit road goes from the summit to the city plaza. Total return ~13 min.

5 — Summit Observation Platform (NEW 7TH CENTERPIECE)

Summit platform — size, material, signage?

The platform is added in v2.0 as the new 7th centerpiece, complementing the 6 binding centerpieces. The default spec is 10×10 wooden platform with spruce-fence railing, 3×3 oak sign panel on a stone-brick pedestal reading "THREE SITES, ONE MOUNTAIN", 360° view. The user may want a different size, a different material, or to skip the 7th centerpiece entirely. Site plan assumes 10×10 oak-plank with spruce-fence railing.

6 — Funicular Grade

Funicular grade: 2:1 (steep) or gentler?

The funicular climbs 600 blocks over ~400 blocks of lateral distance (2:1 grade) on the east or west face of the granite intrusion. A 2:1 grade is steep but matches Swiss funicular precedents (Bürgenstock, Stoos). A gentler grade (e.g., 3:1 or 4:1) would require more lateral distance and a longer rail. Site plan assumes 2:1.

7 — Composite Terrane Plaque Text (geological accuracy)

Should a geologist review the plaque text before final placement?

The plaque text is the geological story of the build. The text references the 1.08 Ga Pikes Peak granite and the 270 Ma Bethany Falls limestone — the real ages of the real rocks. The horizontal contact is real geology (granite plutons push up through limestone in mountain ranges worldwide). The text should be reviewed by a geologist for accuracy before the plaque is finalized.
