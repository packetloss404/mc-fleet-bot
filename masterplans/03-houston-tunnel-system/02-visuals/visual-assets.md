## Key facts pulled from primary sources (use these in the master plan text)

- **Length:** 6+ miles of tunnels, 20 ft (6 m) below grade
- **Coverage:** 95 full city blocks of downtown Houston
- **Daily population:** ~200,000 people on weekdays
- **Hours:** Mon–Fri 6:00 a.m. – 6:00 p.m. (tunnels close evenings & weekends; Theater District links to parking are the exception)
- **Street access:** Only **Wells Fargo Plaza** and **McKinney Garage on Main** offer direct street access; every other entry is via stairs / escalator / elevator inside a private office tower
- **Climate:** Climate-controlled (~72 °F year-round)
- **Depth:** 20 ft below the street
- **Origin (1930s):** The first tunnel connected two downtown movie theaters owned by Will Horwitz (Travis, Texan, Uptown). Another early one ran under Fannin between the Houston Post-Dispatch and the Sterling Building (Ross Sterling). A climate-controlled tunnel between the Downtown Club and the Merchants & Manufacturers Building followed.
- **System idea (1956):** Architectural historian Stephen Fox dates the *system* concept to when the Bank of the Southwest was linked to the 1010 Garage and the Mellie Esperson Building.
- **Tenants:** ~80+ currently listed across the named segments — see Appendix A below.

## 1. Exterior & Approach

The Houston tunnel is unique because the **majority of its entrances are inside private office buildings** — not from the street. Only two direct street-access points exist (Wells Fargo Plaza and McKinney Garage). This is a key visual story for the master plan: an "invisible" network whose life happens one lobby away from the sidewalk.

## 5. Historical

- `historical/1.jpg` (85 KB), `historical/3.jpg` (271 KB), `historical/9.jpg` (421 KB) — 3 quality historical reference images.
- 5 of the 10 originally downloaded files were 404 placeholders (quarantined); the remaining 3 are usable.
- 8 quality files total in this folder after the second round of searches.

**What the master plan should say about history (sources cited above):**
- 1930s: First tunnels were between movie theaters (Horwitz: Travis, Texan, Uptown) and between newspaper/office buildings (Sterling). Climate-controlled tunnels appeared shortly after, connecting the Downtown Club to the Merchants & Manufacturers Building.
- 1956: The Bank of the Southwest ↔ 1010 Garage ↔ Mellie Esperson Building linkage is what architectural historian Stephen Fox calls the origin of the *system* idea.
- 1960s–1980s: Most of the office-tower-connected tunnels were built alongside the towers themselves, each tower's developer extending a stub that the next tower's developer would meet. The tunnels are owned by each tower's landlord — there is no single owner. (The Harris County tunnel is a separately-owned civic cluster.)
- Post-2001 (Tropical Storm Allison): A major flood event in June 2001 sent water cascading into multiple tunnel sections through parking-garage ramps, costing millions. The system added floodgates and many entrances were retrofitted with raised thresholds.

**Suggested use in master plan:** a one-paragraph "how it grew" sidebar, plus one small historical photo (1930s–1950s) if available.

## 9. Gaps — what couldn't be found

1. **High-res food court photos under a free license.** Only one image of the actual Esperson food court is referenced by name (the tenant cover on downtownhouston.org), and it is not redistributable. The Visual Renderer will need to synthesize a food court scene.
2. **A canonical cross-section diagram of the tunnel's structural depth.** The Houston tunnel is privately built and there's no public architectural section drawing. Reference: Wikipedia and the houstontx.gov page both note 20 ft depth and climate control, but no section drawing exists in the public domain. The Visual Renderer will need to use the Wikimedia interior photo + the 20-ft depth fact to construct a representative section.
3. **Tenant-by-tenant interior photography.** Apart from the high-res Wikimedia shot of a typical corridor, there is no public-domain photo tour of specific tenant spaces. The downtownhouston.org listing has tenant cover photos that are restricted.
4. **1930s/1940s archival photos of the original tunnel-between-theaters.** The first tunnels predated mass amateur photography; what little exists is in the Houston Public Library's archival collection (not freely licensed).
5. **Detailed Houston-specific wayfinding signage examples.** The "T-marker" surface sign exists but is small and rarely photographed; nothing in the batch is a clear close-up.
6. **High-res above-grade skybridge specific to Houston.** Most skybridge photos in the batch are generic modern office-district examples. There is no canonical "Houston skybridge" reference photo under a free license.
