# SubTropolis — Discussion Notes

> **Discussion Moderator's deliberation record for the SubTropolis Minecraft replica build.**
> Documented panel: Realist, Gameplay Advocate, Architect, Operations Manager.
> Outcome: binding decisions for the Site Planner, Architectural Designer, and AI Contractor Writer.
> Companion to `01-research/research-report.md` and `01-research/references.md`.
> The visual-assets catalog (`02-visuals/visual-assets.md`) was not yet available at the time of this deliberation; the panel worked from the research report only.

---

## 1. The Panel

This deliberation decides what a Minecraft replica of Hunt Midwest SubTropolis — the world's largest underground business complex, carved into 270-million-year-old Bethany Falls limestone north of the Missouri River — should *be*. The real facility is unrenderable at any literal scale: 1,100 surface acres, 55 million square feet of mined void, 14 million leasable, ~10 miles of paved internal road, more than 10,000 limestone pillars, 55+ tenants, 2,500+ employees, 65–70 °F year-round, no weather, no radio, no pests. The build has to compress all of that into something a player can walk across in 10 minutes and still recognize as *SubTropolis* on a first visit. Four voices argued that question: a **Realist** who wants only what's documented, a **Gameplay Advocate** who wants it traversable, an **Architect** who wants one unified composition, and an **Operations Manager** who wants it to feel like a place where people actually work. They disagreed in the right ways, and the decisions below reflect that disagreement resolved.

## 2. Deliberation Transcript

### Topic 1 — Scale Compression

**The question.** At 1 block = 1 meter, the floor area is ~5.1 million m² — unbuildable. The research recommends a ~200 × 200 block surface footprint, vertical 1:1, 200–400 blocks of main avenue, 1:1 pillars at iconic intersections. Is that the right compression?

**Realist.** The research's numbers are honest: vertical 1:1 is the only dimension that absolutely cannot be touched, because the 16-ft ceiling, the 8×8 pillars, and the 65-ft pillar-to-pillar spacing *are* the visual identity. Compress floor area aggressively — that's the right call — but keep the proportions honest. If a player can see from a distance that the columns look like columns and the ceiling looks like a ceiling, the rest of the scale distortion is forgivable. I would *not* go smaller than 200 × 200; the research already calls that "an iconic microcosm," and below that the place stops reading as a city and starts reading as a basement.

**Gameplay Advocate.** 200 × 200 is fine on paper but in Minecraft that's a 4-minute sprint corner-to-corner. If we want a player to *visit* every tenant zone, we need to either compress further (150 × 150) or design so the corners aren't empty. I'll take 200 × 200 only if the corners have something — a rail spur, an unfinished chamber, an EPA checkpoint — not dead rock. Also, the 300–400-block main avenue has to feel like a single drive-through, not a slog. 350 blocks of main avenue, two side spurs of ~100 blocks each, total walkable.

**Architect.** The 200 × 200 is the right footprint because of the 8×8 pillar geometry. If we shrink the footprint, the pillar grid compresses and the ceiling-to-pillar ratio breaks — the place starts to look like a Roman crypt, not a working industrial complex. 1:1 vertical is non-negotiable. The *length* of the main avenue is where I have a preference: it should run the long axis of the available ravine wall so the second portal lands on a perpendicular face. That gives a true drive-through geometry and lets the column grid recede into one-point perspective — which is the iconic SubTropolis photograph.

**Operations Manager.** Forget the block count; the question is whether a visitor feels the *scale* of the place without having to walk it. Two things sell the scale: (1) sight lines — a player at one end of the main avenue should be able to see pillars and ceiling lights fading into haze at the other end; and (2) the *back-of-house* depth — the unfinished chambers, the "DANGER — Active Mining" dead ends, the dark spurs. I'll accept any footprint as long as I can see a long way down a corridor and there are 2–3 visibly unfinished chambers I can't reach. 200 × 200 with a 350-block main avenue and 2 unfinished dead-end spurs is the right call.

**Tension.** Realist and Architect want the larger 200 × 200 to preserve the urban-grid feel. Gameplay wants smaller for walkability. The compromise is footprint-vs.-density: keep 200 × 200 but *fill* the corners so the Gameplay Advocate's objection is addressed.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The four are nearly unanimous on the critical point: 1:1 vertical, 8×8 pillars, 5-block ceiling. The disagreement is footprint size and corner treatment. The research's recommendation of 200 × 200 with 1:1 vertical holds. The corner-dead-space objection from the Gameplay Advocate is real and must be solved at the design stage, not the discussion stage.

**Decision (binding).**
- **Surface footprint**: ~200 × 200 blocks (target ±20 blocks), shaped to the ravine wall geometry.
- **Vertical scale**: 1:1 — 5-block (16 ft) ceiling, 8×8-block pillars, 12-block corridor width.
- **Main avenue length**: ~350 blocks, oriented along the long axis of the available ravine wall.
- **Side spurs**: two ~100-block spurs branching off the main avenue to house secondary tenant types and a "DANGER — Active Mining" unfinished chamber each.
- **Compression ratio**: approximately 1:100 floor area, 1:1 vertical, 1:1 pillar grid at iconic intersections (relax to 30–40 block pillar-to-pillar spacing in non-iconic zones, per research §10.2).
- **No "big cavern"**: every chamber must read as a segment of the orthogonal grid, never as a single open room.

### Topic 2 — Must-Haves vs. Cuts

**The question.** The research lists 12 must-haves (§10.3). The panel must commit to 8–12 *binding* must-haves, name what gets cut entirely, and identify what gets a "faded reference" (a single nod, not a full build).

**Realist.** I will defend every item on the research list that is publicly documented. The horizontal portal in a hillside, the pillared corridor grid, the geology-named street signs, the 15 mph speed limit, the painted pillar numbers, the constant overhead lighting, the multi-tenant mix, the data center, the unfinished "ghost" mine, the second portal, the constant climate, and the surface parking are all *the site*. I would not cut any. If the build has to drop one to save space, it should be the rail spur — it's real, but it's secondary to the experience.

**Gameplay Advocate.** Every must-have should be either a *visual* or a *navigation* hook. The visual hooks: pillar grid, second portal, geology-named street signs, the unfinished ghost mine, the constant overhead lighting. The navigation hooks: the entrance portal, the multi-tenant mix, the through-route between two portals. I'll demote the surface parking to a small lot (the visual is generic), and the rail spur to either a few parked boxcars in a dead-end spur or nothing. The data center, the USPS loading dock, the NARA archive — those are the tenant anchors; they need to be in the build, fully realized.

**Architect.** The pillar grid *is* the silhouette. If you remove it, you have a generic underground warehouse. Everything else is supporting cast. I'd organize the must-haves in three tiers: (1) load-bearing — pillar grid, two portals, main avenue; (2) signature — geology-named street signs, painted pillar numbers, multi-tenant variety, unfinished ghost chamber; (3) flavor — the rest. The rail spur is a tier-3 thing at best, and only if there's room.

**Operations Manager.** A place where people work has docks. SubTropolis has 500+ truck docks. A build that doesn't have *visible* dock doors — semi-trucks pulling up to a tenant entrance — is a build that doesn't respect the operation. I'm pushing hard for at least one tenant entrance with a real dock door, a real trailer backed in, dock leveler, dock lights. Same for the data center: it should look *operational* — server racks, not a "tech room" with a sign. I would demote the surface parking (a parking lot is generic) and the rail spur (one or two parked cars on a short track is plenty).

**Tension.** Realist wants all 12. Architect and Operations Manager both want the rail spur cut to a minimum. The deeper tension is between *coverage* (Realist, Architect) and *depth* (Operations Manager, Gameplay) — should we build 12 features at moderate fidelity, or 8 features at high fidelity?

**Moderator's synthesis.** The depth-over-coverage argument wins. The research already explicitly recommends "8–12 tenant types and label the rest with a 'Vacant / Mining Crew' sign" (§10.4). The rail spur is the natural sacrifice: it's real but not iconic. The pillar grid + two portals + tenant variety + the unfinished ghost chamber form the irreducible core.

**Decision (binding).**

| Tier | Must-haves (binding) |
|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Load-bearing** | (a) Horizontal portal entrance in a hillside, with a paved ramp at shallow grade. (b) Pillared corridor grid: 8×8 pillars, 12-block corridor, 5-block ceiling, on a regular orthogonal grid. (c) Second portal at the far end of the main avenue, enabling the through-route. |
| **Tier 2 — Signature** | (d) Geology-themed street names (Hushpuckney at minimum, plus 1–2 invented companions documented as such). (e) Painted pillar numbers at every intersection. (f) 15 mph speed limit and stop signs posted on the main avenue. (g) Multi-tenant variety: at least 6 visibly distinct tenant fit-outs along the main avenue, with at least one dock-door scene. (h) Unfinished "ghost" mine chamber off a side spur — dark, raw rock, "DANGER — Active Mining" signage, deliberately unreachable. |
| **Tier 3 — Flavor** | (i) SubTropolis Technology Center (data center) with biometric entry, visible server racks, red/green status lights. (j) Constant-climate / no-weather / no-mob-deeper feel conveyed through environmental design. (k) Surface plateau with Hunt Midwest visitor center, parking lot stub, and a Lamar Hunt / KC Chiefs plaque (easter egg, see Topic 5). |

**Cuts (no build).** Full surface parking lot, the 2,500 surface acres, the amusement park above (single tower silhouette at most, per research §10.4), the full 10.5 miles of road, the 7.5 M sq ft of expansion capacity, the 1998 Ford 25-acre vehicle-storage lease as a named exhibit (the Ford story belongs in lore, not in space).

**Faded references (one nod, not a build).** The 2,500-acre surface landholding (one block of "above-ground stuff" with a single tower and the Worlds of Fun marquee silhouette visible in the distance from the ravine rim). The Hunt/Chiefs ownership (one plaque in a "Hunt Hall" plaza, per Topic 5). The foreign-trade zone designation (one sign in the leasing office).

### Topic 3 — Source Conflicts

**The question.** The research flags six real conflicts: total area (55 M vs 14 M vs 150 M sq ft), ceiling height (15–25 ft vs 30 ft), depth (100–150 ft vs 160 ft max), tenant count (200+ vs 55), opening date (1964 vs "the 1960s"), and the various temperature claims. What does the build canon use?

**Realist.** Pick the most-cited *and* most-corroborated figure for each, and use the next-most-cited as a secondary sign. Concretely: 55 million sq ft (this is the figure the public knows; the 14 M is a leasable figure that doesn't appear in any visitor-facing signage), 160 ft deep (the *maximum*; most of the complex is shallower), 16 ft ceiling (the standard corridor; 17–20 ft is the new-build STC), 55+ tenants (current), 1964 (specific year, documented). On the temperature, 65–70 °F is the range; pick 68 °F as the displayed figure (a midpoint, no source disputes the upper 60s).

**Gameplay Advocate.** I don't care which numbers are "true" — I care which numbers make the build feel right. 55 M is the famous number. Use 55 M. The 14 M can appear on a "FLOOR AREA LEASED" sign inside the leasing office. Ceiling: 16 ft is the right proportion; the build should *display* "16 FT CLEAR" at the corridor mouth. Depth: 150 ft, not 160, because it reads more cleanly. Tenants: "55+" on the marquee sign.

**Architect.** The numbers should be deployed in service of composition. The number at the entrance is 55 M — it's the *claim*, the thing the public associates with SubTropolis. The number inside the leasing office is 14 M — it's the *fact*, the operational reality. The number on the deepest-chamber sign is 150 ft. This is a hierarchy: marketing claim at the threshold, technical fact in the back office, and depth as a spatial cue where it matters.

**Operations Manager.** I'm a building manager. The number my leasing team would quote on the phone is 55 million square feet of *mined-out* space and 14 million of *developed* leasable. The 55 M is the headline because it makes the caller's eyes widen; the 14 M is what I quote when I send a contract. Both are correct. In the build, the entrance sign should say **55 million sq ft**; the leasing office plaque should say **14 million sq ft developed**. The depth figure on the visitor-center wall should say **up to 160 feet below surface** — that's the legal/operational language; the 100–150 ft number is the *where you'll be standing* number. 1964 is the founding year; that's the date on the "SubTropolis / Est. 1964" plaque.

**Tension.** Realist and Architect both want a hierarchy of numbers. The Gameplay Advocate just wants memorability. The Operations Manager is essentially the Architect's argument in operational clothing. There is no real conflict — only one disagreement, which is whether the *secondary* number (14 M developed) deserves its own sign or only appears in the master plan.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The 55 M vs 14 M distinction is the same distinction the *Atlantic*, the ULI study, and Wikipedia all make (§8 of the research, "Public Facts vs. Myth / Marketing"). The build should honor it with a two-tier signage hierarchy. The other conflicts are all resolved by a single canonical choice, with the variant numbers appearing only in the master plan as "documented alternative" notes.

**Decision (binding).**

| Conflicting claim | Build canon | Where it appears | Alternative numbers (master plan only) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total area | **55 million sq ft** | Entrance marquee sign, leasing office plaque | 14 M sq ft developed (secondary sign in leasing office); 150+ M "mined" appears in the research report as marketing, not in the build |
| Ceiling height | **16 ft (5 blocks at 1:1)** | Posted at corridor mouths, on leasing spec sheets | 17–20 ft noted on SubTropolis Technology Center signage |
| Depth below surface | **150 ft** (the *representative* depth for the visitor experience) | Visitor-center interpretive wall, deepest-chamber interpretive sign | "Up to 160 ft" noted on the same wall as the maximum; the limestone shelf "50–100 ft below surface" noted in the master plan only |
| Tenant count | **55+** | Marketing sign, leasing office tenant list | 200+ figure (which appears in some sources) is treated as a marketing number, not used |
| Opening year | **1964** | "SubTropolis — Est. 1964" plaque at the entrance, on the Hunt Hall exhibit | 1970 Great Midwest purchase is a secondary date on the same plaque; 1985 Hunt Midwest Real Estate Development restructuring is a tertiary line |
| Temperature | **68 °F** | Visitor-center wall text | 65–70 °F range noted on the same wall |
| Energy savings | **50–70%** | Visitor-center sustainability exhibit | 90% is a marketing claim, not used in interpretive copy |
| Road length | **8.2 mi** | Interpretive wall (the paved-arterial figure) | 10.5 mi / 13 mi noted as "including spurs and access roads" in master plan only |
| Rail length | **2.1 mi** | Master plan and rail-spur signage | — |
| Compressive strength | **18,000–24,000 psi** | Visitor-center geology exhibit | — |

### Topic 4 — Creative License Boundaries

**The question.** Most SubTropolis tenant interiors are private and not photographed. Where is creative license allowed? How does the master plan mark "this is documented" vs "this is designer's interpretation"?

**Realist.** The default should be "do not invent." Anything that can't be sourced from Wikipedia, Hunt Midwest, the ULI study, the 2010 *Atlantic* piece, the 2012 Hunt press release, or the 2024–2026 reporting is *not* in the build. The federal tenants (USPS, NARA, EPA) are documented enough to be built with confidence. The Hallmark packaging, the Russell Stover candy storage, the Pillsbury flour storage — those are documented as *activities* but not as *layouts*, so the interior layout is invention. Mark every invention with a small italicized "designer interpretation" note in the master plan; do not mark individual builds (the player doesn't need to know which is which).

**Gameplay Advocate.** The player doesn't read the master plan. The player sees corridors, dock doors, racking, server racks, products. As long as the *interior feel* matches what an industrial storage facility looks like, the player will believe it. Invent freely, but invent with conviction — half-built shelves, half-stocked racks, real product types. Empty rooms with just a sign are the worst possible choice; they tell the player "we ran out of ideas."

**Architect.** Visual coherence demands a design language for tenant fit-outs. The fit-outs should follow a small set of archetypes — say, four: "long-term records archive" (NARA), "package logistics" (USPS), "data center" (STC), "general industrial" (Hallmark, Russell Stover, generic e-commerce). Each archetype has a defined color palette, fixture set, and a representative interior. A tenant that doesn't fit an archetype gets the "general industrial" archetype with a tenant-specific sign. This is *coherent invention*, not free invention.

**Operations Manager.** The way to invent a tenant interior is to ask: "what does this tenant *do* all day?" A USPS stamp distribution center has boxes of stamps, conveyor belts, package sorting, loading docks. A NARA federal records center has shelving 30 feet high, climate sensors, and a curator's office. A Grainger industrial supply distribution has pallet racking, conveyor belts, a pick-and-pack station, and shipping manifests on whiteboards. The *activity* of the building is what an Operations Manager notices, and that's what an industrial worker recognizes. Invent the *activity*, not just the *objects*.

**Tension.** Realist wants a strict "documented only" default. Architect and Operations Manager want coherent invention. Gameplay wants conviction in execution. The compromise is a *legend* in the master plan and a *standard of invention* in the design.

**Decision (binding).**

- **Master plan legend (mandatory).** Every build element in the master plan is tagged with one of three labels:
  - `[D]` = Documented in the research report (cite section).
  - `[I]` = Inferred from industry standard for the tenant type (cite the archetype).
  - `[X]` = Invented by the designer to fill a gap (describe the rationale).
- **Standard of invention.** An invented interior must (1) match a documented tenant's actual business activity, (2) follow one of four defined fit-out archetypes (records archive / package logistics / data center / general industrial), and (3) be "operationally plausible" — meaning, if a former SubTropolis employee walked in, they would not laugh.
- **What is fully documented and may be built without legend tags:** the pillar grid, the 16-ft ceiling, the 8×8 pillars on 65-ft centers, the 15 mph speed limit, the geology-themed street names, the second portal, the constant climate, the Hunt/Chiefs ownership, the 1964 opening, the 500+ dock door count (as a *number*, not a build), the USPS, NARA, EPA, LightEdge, Grainger, Hallmark, Russell Stover, Pillsbury, Underground Vaults & Storage, and Ford tenant names, the 102,000 sq ft NARA initial lease, the SubTropolis Technology Center specifications (biometric, ENERGY STAR 100, dark-fiber to 1102 Grand), and the Derrough Drive north-side expansion.
- **What is inferred ([I] tag) and may be built as a single representative example:** a typical 17-ft data-center fit-out, a typical NARA records archive shelving configuration, a typical USPS stamp-distribution conveyor layout, a typical Grainger fulfillment pick-and-pack line.
- **What is invented ([X] tag) and may be built as needed:** the specific corridor between NARA and USPS, the interior of the "Vacant / Mining Crew" chambers, the un-documented tenants (those not named above), the parking lot layout, the rail spur, the unfinished ghost mine's interior details.

### Topic 5 — Easter Eggs and Cultural References

**The question.** SubTropolis is associated in the public mind with: "everything you wanted to store in a cave," the USPS connection, the climate-controlled records storage, the e-commerce / Amazon-adjacent fulfillment, the Hunt/Chiefs family. The panel must decide which easter eggs appear and how explicit they are. The Realist has been clear about resisting Indiana Jones / Batcave / Bond villain contamination.

**Realist.** Three rules: (1) no torches, no idols, no treasure, no hidden lair imagery — anything that evokes Indiana Jones or a villain's secret base is out; (2) easter eggs must be *referenced in the documented history*, not invented; (3) the Hunt/Chiefs reference is allowed because Lamar Hunt is the documented founder, but it should be a dignified plaque, not a stadium. The Wizard of Oz / Gone with the Wind film cans are widely cited but I have not found a primary source confirming the exact inventory. Treat as plausibly-true, not confirmed. Build the film archive as a generic film vault, not as a Wizard of Oz exhibit.

**Gameplay Advocate.** The players love easter eggs. The Hunt/Chiefs reference should be visible but tasteful — a single Chiefs-color accent on a sign, a "Hunt Hall" round room, a Lamar Hunt bust. The film archive should be a "faded reference" — a small room with a sign that says "Underground Vaults & Storage — film masters on file" and a few film canisters on a shelf. The "World's Largest Underground Business Complex" trademark should be at the entrance, big. A few genuine "the ceiling is 16 ft and the rock is 270 million years old" interpretive signs feel like easter eggs to the curious player.

**Architect.** Easter eggs must *compose*. A Chiefs helmet on a wall is a poster, not architecture. A Lamar Hunt bronze in a small round room at the entry to a plaza is a *place*. A "Hunt Hall" with a single dignified bust, a single mounted Chiefs arrowhead, and a small interpretive wall about the Hunt family's role in KC — that is a *room with content*, not a pinboard. The film archive should be a vault with rows of film cans on shelves, lit only by safelights, with one shelf having an unmarked can and a small label that reads "GONE WITH THE WIND — MASTER" — that is a *scene*, not a display.

**Operations Manager.** I worked in a facility like this. The things that *aren't* in any press release are the things the workers remember: the constant hum of the HVAC, the smell of cardboard and dust, the security guard at the entrance, the truck driver radio, the way the morning shift queues at the security gate, the cafeteria (yes, there is a cafeteria, somewhere). Those are the easter eggs I want. Not movie references. Not Chiefs helmets. The *work*.

**Tension.** Realist and Operations Manager want restraint and dignity. Gameplay wants visible easter eggs. Architect wants the easter eggs to be *places* not *posters*. The natural resolution: make the easter eggs a small number of *named places* (Hunt Hall, the UV&S vault, the data-center biometric entry) rather than a sprinkling of *hidden references*.

**Decision (binding).**

- **Prominent (clearly labeled, player-facing):**
  - **USPS Stamp Distribution / Fulfillment Center** — labeled, with a visible dock door scene (semi-trailer backed in, dock leveler, package conveyor inside). Most-documented marquee tenant; the public already associates SubTropolis with USPS.
  - **NARA Federal Records Center** — labeled, with a visible high-bay records shelving interior.
  - **SubTropolis Technology Center** — labeled, with biometric hand-reader prop, dark-fiber conduit detail, red/green status lights on visible server racks.
  - **EPA Region 7 Training & Logistics Center** — labeled, smaller, emergency-response signage.
  - **"World's Largest Underground Business Complex"®** — at the entrance marquee, large, as it is on the real site (a trademarked phrase; the build should reproduce it because it's part of the visual identity).

- **Subtle / "faded reference" (single nod, not a full build):**
  - **Underground Vaults & Storage (UV&S) film archive** — a single secured room, 30 × 20 blocks, with a "UV&S — Authorized Personnel Only" sign, three rows of metal shelving, a few unlabeled film cans on a shelf, and a single small label reading one of: "GONE WITH THE WIND — INTERPOSITIVE" or "WIZARD OF OZ — MASTER." No movie posters, no green-ray imagery, no display cases. The label is the easter egg.
  - **Lamar Hunt / KC Chiefs** — a single "Hunt Hall" round room (or equivalent) at the central plaza, with a Lamar Hunt interpretive plaque and a small mounted Chiefs arrowhead. No Chiefs helmet, no football imagery, no stadium references. The Hunt family's documented role is the content; the Chiefs is one bullet point, not the theme.
  - **Foreign-trade zone** — a single sign in the leasing office: "Designated U.S. Foreign-Trade Zone — Largest Under One Roof."
  - **ENERGY STAR 100** — a single framed certificate on the leasing office wall (visual gag, low-build).

- **Out (zero build, no reference):**
  - Indiana Jones, Batcave, Bond villain, secret lair, or any "hidden treasure" imagery.
  - Torch-lit corridors, fake idols, ancient traps, hidden chambers with rewards.
  - Wizards, dragons, magic, mob spawners, or any fantasy contamination.
  - Movie posters for *Wizard of Oz* or *Gone with the Wind*. The film cans alone are the reference; the films themselves are not promoted.
  - Anything that requires the player to know a specific pop-culture reference to appreciate the build.

- **Atmospheric easter eggs (the Operations Manager's contribution):**
  - The corridor ambient sound is the Minecraft cave ambient (which already has a "muffled" feel).
  - A working-clock-style set of "shift change" indicators (signs that read "FIRST SHIFT — 06:00" / "SECOND SHIFT — 14:00" / "THIRD SHIFT — 22:00").
  - A small "SubTropolis Employee Cafeteria" room with a few tables, a coffee station, a vending machine block.
  - A security guard booth at the entrance with a "Check In" sign and a clipboard prop.

### Topic 6 — Combined-Complex Integration

**The question.** The project brief places SubTropolis on the south side of a deep ravine/gorge, opposite Cheyenne Mountain Complex on the north side. A service tunnel runs through the bottom of the ravine connecting the two. A public shaft runs from SubTropolis up to the city on the valley floor. How do these inter-site features manifest in the SubTropolis build?

**Realist.** Both the service tunnel and the public shaft are *project fictions* — they don't exist in the real SubTropolis. The build must mark them as such, both in-world (with interpretive signage) and in the master plan (with `[X]` tags). The public shaft especially: in the real SubTropolis, workers arrive by car on a private drive; there is no public transit, no pedestrian shaft, no vertical access. The fiction should be a *discrete addition* to the canonical SubTropolis, not a re-imagining of the facility.

**Gameplay Advocate.** The whole point of the combined-complex project is that the player walks between the three sites. The service tunnel and the public shaft are how they do it. Both need to be *player-traversable*, not just lore tunnels. The service tunnel should support minecarts (the natural Minecraft vehicle for a long underground trip). The public shaft should support a player taking a boat, an elevator, or a ladder up to the city on the valley floor. Both are part of the player experience, not just world-building.

**Architect.** The terminus matters. The service tunnel and the public shaft both need to *enter the SubTropolis grid at a point that makes spatial sense*. The service tunnel, being a long horizontal passage at the bottom of the ravine, should enter at a sub-basement level — below the main grid, accessed by a downward stair or by a corridor that steps down. The public shaft, being a vertical passage, should land at a small "lobby" or "security checkpoint" with a clear vertical line that the player can see going up. The terminus on the SubTropolis side should be at a *less-developed* edge of the grid (not the heart of Hunt Hall) so the fiction doesn't disrupt the canonical composition.

**Operations Manager.** A service tunnel between two underground facilities means *maintenance vehicles*, not pedestrians. It should be designed for a small service vehicle (a John Deere Gator, a Cushman, a mine-spec buggy) — single-lane, with a turnaround on the SubTropolis side. There should be a *security gate* between the tunnel and the SubTropolis grid, because SubTropolis is private property and a public-shaft connection would be a real security concern in the real world. The public shaft should have a staffed entry — a security guard booth, a check-in desk, perhaps a turnstile — because a facility that is normally access-controlled cannot have a public entrance without that.

**Tension.** Realist wants the fiction clearly marked. Gameplay wants traversability. Architect wants spatial coherence. Operations Manager wants operational realism (security gates, vehicle turnarounds). These are all compatible if the fictions are *integrated as designed additions* rather than ignored or over-stylized.

**Moderator's synthesis.** The service tunnel and public shaft are *project fictions* that the panel endorses, with clear in-world signage. The terminus design must respect the SubTropolis grid composition (Architect), enable gameplay traversal (Gameplay), and reflect operational security norms (Operations Manager). The Realist's requirement to mark them as fictions is satisfied by interpretive signage and master-plan tags.

**Decision (binding).**

- **Service tunnel (project fiction, marked in-world):**
  - **Path:** horizontal, single-lane, ~6 blocks wide × 5 blocks high, running the length of the ravine bottom from a Cheyenne Mountain maintenance sub-basement to a SubTropolis service sub-basement.
  - **SubTropolis terminus:** a small service sub-basement *below* the main SubTropolis grid, accessed from the main grid by a downward corridor with a security gate. The sub-basement contains a small vehicle turnaround, a maintenance bay, and signage reading "SERVICE TUNNEL — COMBINED COMPLEX MAINTENANCE — AUTHORIZED VEHICLES ONLY." The downward corridor is integrated into the SubTropolis composition at a less-developed edge (e.g., near the unfinished ghost mine chamber).
  - **Vehicle support:** minecart-compatible (for the player) and visually designed for a small service vehicle (a parked utility cart, a single-vehicle bay, vehicle tracks on the floor).
  - **Master plan tag:** `[X]` — Project fiction, not in documented SubTropolis history.

- **Public shaft (project fiction, marked in-world):**
  - **Path:** vertical, ~5 × 5 blocks, running from the SubTropolis grid up through the ravine wall to a surface lobby on the valley floor.
  - **SubTropolis terminus:** a "Public Access Lobby" at the edge of the main grid, with a security guard booth, a turnstile, a "Public Transit — Combined Complex" sign, and a vertical shaft (visible) going straight up. The shaft is *visible from the lobby* (a long vertical line, perhaps with a player-climbable ladder or a minecraft elevator) so the player understands the geometry.

## 4. Tensions and Tradeoffs Acknowledged

The panel made real choices, and the choices have costs. The following are the things the build will *not* be, as a consequence of the decisions above:

- **We will not be a literal-scale replica.** At ~1:100 floor compression, the build is roughly 1% of the real facility. A purist who walks the build will not feel they have walked SubTropolis. They will feel they have walked a *representative slice* — and that is the panel's call. The Realist accepts this in exchange for a buildable, visitable build.
- **We will not honor the 1998 Ford 25-acre vehicle-storage lease as a named exhibit.** The Ford lease is a real part of the history but the panel chose to give the historical floor space to Hunt/Chiefs (a single plaque) and to current marquee tenants (full builds). This is a deliberate tradeoff: documented history loses space to documented current use.
- **We will not show the full "mole people" social-history dimension of SubTropolis.** The 1998 *Asian Wall Street Journal* "Mole People of Kansas City" framing is a documented and humane part of the SubTropolis story, but the panel judged it too narrative-heavy for a build that the Operations Manager wants to feel like a place of *work*, not a *documentary subject*. The Operations Manager's framing wins.
- **We will not show the amusement parks above ground.** Worlds of Fun and Oceans of Fun sit directly above the real SubTropolis. Showing them would add significant build scope and dilute the "underground" focus. A single tower silhouette is the compromise.
- **The rail spur is reduced to a few parked cars on a short track (or omitted).** This is a real loss; the real SubTropolis has 2.1 miles of rail. The Gameplay Advocate and Architect argued for a full rail line, but the Operations Manager and Realist argued for dock doors instead. Docks won.
- **The Hunt/Chiefs reference is a single plaque.** A sports franchise is a documented part of the Hunt legacy, but a single plaque is all the build will have. A sports-themed "fan zone" is out — that would distort the canonical composition.
- **The 14 M sq ft "developed" number is less prominent than the 55 M "mined-out" number.** This is honest — the 55 M is the public-facing number — but it does mean a player who knows the industry will notice that the build's headline number is the marketing claim, not the operational fact. The leasing office plaque and the master plan cover this; the player-facing signage does not.
- **The combined-complex service tunnel and public shaft are *project fictions*.** They are clearly marked as such, but they are not in the documented SubTropolis history. A purist who knows the real facility will notice the lobby, the turnstile, the downward corridor to a sub-basement that doesn't exist in the real world. The trade is interoperability with Cheyenne Mountain and Houston — the fictions are the price of the combined-complex project.

## 5. Open Questions for the User

The panel has decided what it can decide. The following are the questions the panel flagged for the human / architect that the deliberation could not resolve:

1. **Geology-themed street name companions.** The research confirms "Hushpuckney" as one geology-themed street name. The full list is not public. The panel has approved 1–2 invented companions *if they are documented as invented* `[X]`. The architect should propose the names (e.g., Iola, Bethany Falls, Winterset, Galesburg, Hushpuckney, Mound City — these are real Bethany Falls member / Kansas City-area geologic terms) and have them approved before sign placement.

2. **Surface plateau composition.** The build needs a small surface plateau with a Hunt Midwest visitor center, a parking lot stub, and a Lamar Hunt / KC Chiefs plaque. The architect should decide how much of the ravine rim to render and whether to include a single Worlds-of-Fun silhouette in the distance. The panel did not decide the size of the surface plateau.

3. **The "Hunt Hall" round room location.** The panel agreed on the *concept* of a small "Hunt Hall" round room at the central plaza, with a Lamar Hunt interpretive plaque. The architect should decide the exact location, the size (panel suggests ~30 × 30 blocks), and the rotation of the room. The panel did not specify location because the ravine geometry is the architect's domain.

4. **Data center detail depth.** The STC is in the build (Tier 3 must-have). The Operations Manager wants visible server racks, status lights, biometric hand-reader prop, dark-fiber conduit. The architect should confirm the level of detail (e.g., is each server rack a single decoration block, or are the racks 4–6 blocks deep with multiple rack units?). The panel left the detail to the design team because it is a design-stage question, not a discussion-stage one.

5. **Inter-site coordination with the Cheyenne Mountain and Houston tunnel-system teams.** The service tunnel cross-section (~6 × 5 blocks) and the public shaft cross-section (~5 × 5 blocks) need to be confirmed with the other two build teams. The SubTropolis end is specified above; the panel did not specify the other ends.

6. **Operations Manager question for the human: should the build include a NARA-style "high-bay records shelving" interior that goes nearly to the ceiling?** The Operations Manager argued for it (a 30-ft tall shelving configuration is a defining visual of an archive), but the panel agreed it is the architect's call whether the ceiling-to-shelving proportion is correct in Minecraft. The architect should decide based on the visitor-experience composition.

These six questions are flagged for the architect and the human project lead. The panel did not block on them; the decisions above are binding even before these are resolved.

## 6. Combined-Complex Coordination Notes

The following are the inter-site decisions the combined-complex team (Cheyenne Mountain, SubTropolis, Houston tunnel system) will need to honor. These are restated from the Topic 6 decision and the relevant D-rows above, for ease of reference.

- **SubTropolis is on the south side of the ravine.** The portal entrance is on the south ravine wall, with a paved ramp at shallow grade from a surface plateau (the Hunt Midwest visitor center + parking lot stub).
- **The service tunnel runs through the bottom of the ravine**, horizontal, ~6 × 5 blocks, single-lane, vehicle-traversable (minecart for the player, designed for a small service vehicle in the lore). It connects a Cheyenne Mountain maintenance sub-basement to a SubTropolis service sub-basement.
- **The SubTropolis service tunnel terminus** is *below* the main grid (sub-basement), accessed by a downward corridor with a security gate. The terminus is at a less-developed edge of the grid, near the unfinished ghost mine chamber.
- **The public shaft is vertical**, ~5 × 5 blocks, running from a "Public Access Lobby" at the SubTropolis grid edge up through the ravine wall to a surface lobby on the valley floor. The SubTropolis end has a security guard booth, a turnstile, a visible vertical shaft.
- **Both the service tunnel and the public shaft are *project fictions*, tagged `[X]` in the SubTropolis master plan and in the combined-complex report.** They are clearly marked as not-in-documented-history at the terminus design (interpretive signage) and in the master plan.
- **Cross-section compatibility.** The service tunnel is ~6 × 5 blocks (SubTropolis end). The public shaft is ~5 × 5 blocks. The Cheyenne Mountain and Houston tunnel-system teams should match these cross-sections at their respective ends.
- **SubTropolis is the "open to the public, mixed use" leg of the trilogy** (per research §13, handoff note 6). Cheyenne Mountain is military / secure / single-use; Houston is pedestrian / downtown; SubTropolis is the suburban industrial / civilian / commercial leg. The combined-complex map should reflect this distinction: SubTropolis as the *most publicly accessible* of the three, with the public shaft as the explicit public-transit entry, in contrast to Cheyenne Mountain's secure perimeter.
