# SubTropolis — Research Report

> **Hunt Midwest SubTropolis**, the world's largest underground business complex.
> Carved into the 270-million-year-old Bethany Falls limestone deposit, bluffs north of the Missouri River, Kansas City, Missouri.
> Research package for the Minecraft replica project (SubTropolis site on the south side of a deep ravine/gorge in the same mountain range as Cheyenne Mountain).
> Prepared by the Research Lead. Replica-first build; creative license documented where applied.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

SubTropolis is a 14-million-square-foot industrial and commercial complex built inside an active room-and-pillar limestone mine on the bluffs above the Missouri River in northern Kansas City, Missouri. Hunt Midwest — the real-estate arm of the Lamar Hunt family — has been leasing the underground since 1964, and trademarked the phrase **"World's Largest Underground Business Complex"**. The site is approximately 1,100 acres (4.5 km², ~1.7 sq mi) of subsurface void space carved out of the Bethany Falls member of the Swope Limestone; today it contains ~10.5 mi (16.9 km) of paved illuminated roads, several miles of rail, more than 10,000 load-bearing pillars, 500+ truck docks, and roughly 55 tenants (USPS, NARA, EPA, Ford vehicle storage historically, LightEdge, Grainger, Hallmark, Underground Vaults & Storage, Russell Stover, Pillsbury, etc.). The temperature holds at 65–70 °F year-round with no humidity, no UV, no weather, and the limestone roof is rated at 18,000–24,000 psi (≈ 6× concrete). The mine is dug up to ~160 ft (49 m) below the surface, with the bulk of leasable space at 100–150 ft depth. Rent runs 30–50 % below comparable above-ground space; the site has held an EPA ENERGY STAR 100 (perfect) warehouse rating since 2012 and reportedly saves 50–90 % in total energy cost. **For the Minecraft replica**, the headline must-haves are: the underground road network with named streets and a 15 mph speed limit, the 25-foot square limestone pillars on 65-foot centers, the constant climate, the multi-tenant industrial mix, the iconic federal-government archival tenants, and the surface entrances cut horizontally into a hillside rather than down a mine shaft. Scale compression is unavoidable: at 1 block = 1 meter the real footprint is on the order of 2,000 m on a side, and the full 10.5 mi of road would be ~16,900 blocks long. The build should treat the SubTropolis core as an iconic *microcosm* — not a literal Minecraft copy of the mine.

## 2. History

### 2.1 The Hunt family and the asphalt-to-real-estate pivot

The site that became SubTropolis was first developed in 1945 as a lateral limestone-mining operation east of present-day I-435 in Clay County, Missouri, by **Midwest Precote**, a regional asphalt producer. Mining continued through the end of the 20th century; the resulting voids are what now house the complex [1, 2, 14].

**Lamar Hunt** — better known as the founder of the AFL, owner of the Kansas City Chiefs, and principal founder of Major League Soccer — was a chief investor in the **Great Midwest Corporation**, which purchased Midwest Precote in 1970 to develop emptied mining sites for commercial use. Before that, Hunt had already personally "pulled the trigger" on SubTropolis in 1964, with the first underground complex opening for commercial leasing that same year, initially for "good and equipment storage" [1, 6, 15].

- **First tenant**: a local construction company, leasing storage space for heavy machinery [1, 6].
- **Early marquee tenants (1960s)**: Pillsbury, Russell Stover Candies, and Ford Motor Company [1, 6, 14].
- **Early 1970s**: Ford Motor Company leased **25 acres** of the facility to store vehicle inventory [1].

### 2.2 Growth, the amusement parks above, and the corporate split

In **1973**, Hunt developed **Worlds of Fun** on a 300-acre site directly above the underground complex, with the **Oceans of Fun** water-park expansion added in 1982 [1, 6]. In 1983, Hunt Midwest reported 3 million square feet of developed commercial space [1].

The Hunt corporate structure reorganized in **1985**: **Hunt Midwest Enterprises** was formed from the merger of Great Midwest Corporation and Mid-America Enterprises, creating three subsidiaries, including Hunt Midwest Mining, Inc. and **Hunt Midwest Real Estate Development, Inc.**, the latter of which took over operation of SubTropolis's commercial holdings [1, 6].

In **1995**, Hunt Midwest sold Worlds of Fun and Oceans of Fun to Cedar Fair Entertainment Company, becoming a partial owner, and seating then-president and CEO Lee Derrough on its board [1, 6]. A subsequent major arterial inside the complex — **Derrough Drive** — is named after him [12].

### 2.3 The 21st century: tech and data centers

- **2001**: After the 2001 anthrax attacks, ~50 employees at the USPS stamp facility in SubTropolis were tested and given precautionary antibiotics [1, 6].
- **2012**: **National Archives and Records Administration (NARA)** signed a lease for 102,000 sq ft of warehouse + office space, with options to expand to 372,000 sq ft; the first project north of Derrough Drive, accessed from Parvin Road [12].
- **2013–2014**: **LightEdge Solutions** announced a **$58 million**, 21-job data-center investment; **FoodServiceWarehouse.com** invested **$1.75 million** for 40 jobs [12].
- **2014**: LightEdge opened a data center in the new "SubTropolis Technology Center" (STC) [1, 9, 12].
- **2026 (planned)**: **W.W. Grainger** expansion set to make the site the largest known underground distribution center in the world [1, 6].

The mine itself is still active. As of the 2010 reporting, Hunt Midwest was still mining fresh chambers on the far edges of its holdings and adding new leasable space at roughly **3.2 acres per year** [6, 7, 12].

### 2.4 The Lamar Hunt legacy

Lamar Hunt died in December 2006. The Hunt family remains one of America's wealthiest dynasties, with marquee entities that include the Kansas City Chiefs, the Chicago Bulls, FC Dallas, the United Center, and Hunt Midwest [1, 12, 14]. An ESPN documentary, *The Kingdom* (Sept. 2025), covers Hunt's role in founding SubTropolis, with a feature article in *Axios Local Kansas City* describing the annual "Groundhog Run" charity race held inside the mine [14, 15].

## 3. Geography & Geology

### 3.1 Location

- **Address**: Hunt Midwest SubTropolis, **8300 NE Underground Drive, Kansas City, MO 64161** [10, 11, 13, 16].
- **Parent office**: Hunt Midwest — Crossroads, 1881 Main Street, Suite 200, Kansas City, MO 64108 [11].
- **Coordinates**: 39.161213° N, 94.476242° W (in the bluffs north of the Missouri River) [1, 6].
- **Locale**: SubTropolis sits in the bluffs north of the Missouri River, opposite downtown KC. The Hunt Midwest site (CLUI geolocation) plots at roughly 39.1576 N, 94.4785 W [2].
- **Travel context**: "10 minutes from Kansas City's central business district" and "20 minutes from Kansas City International Airport" [7].

### 3.2 Geology — the Bethany Falls Limestone

SubTropolis is carved into the **Bethany Falls Limestone**, a member of the **Swope Limestone formation** of Missourian (Upper Pennsylvanian) age, deposited approximately **270 million years ago** in a shallow tropical sea [1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 10]. The Wikipedia article notes the surrounding Kansas City area also contains significant limestone deposits exposed and concentrated by Pleistocene glaciation, which rerouted the Missouri River across northern Kansas [1].

Hunt Midwest's own site summarises: "SubTropolis was created through the lateral mining of a 270-million-year-old limestone deposit" [3]. The roof rock is competent, dry, and self-supporting over very large spans. The mined stratum is described as "nearly level and very deep" [4].

**Engineering properties of the rock** (per Hunt Midwest's TripAdvisor listing and press materials) [8]:
- Compressive strength: **18,000–24,000 psi** — roughly **6× the strength of structural concrete**.
- > **10,000 limestone pillars** remain as structural columns.
- Pillars are 25 ft (7.6 m) square; tunnels are 16 ft (4.9 m) high × 40 ft (12 m) wide, with the pillars on **65-foot centers** [1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 12].

### 3.3 Depth and total footprint

- **Surface footprint**: ~1,100 acres (≈ 4.5 km², ~1.7 sq mi) of underground void; above it lies roughly 2,500 surface acres that include the amusement parks, Hunt Midwest mining/real-estate operations, and a vehicle-auction/storage yard [4, 5, 6, 8, 10].
- **Mined void volume**: marketed as **55 million sq ft** of "mined-out" total volume, of which **14 million sq ft** is developed leasable space — the rest is unfinished or held for future build-out [1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10].
- **Depth**: SubTropolis is "up to 160 ft (49 m) beneath the surface" in its deepest sections [1, 6, 7, 8]. The leasable platform sits at about 100–150 ft down, with the limestone shelf starting 50–100 ft beneath surface soil [4, 8]. Other sources report "150 ft below the surface" as a representative figure [5, 7, 10].
- **Wall between mining and developed space**: the ULI case study notes a wall **65 ft thick** separating the still-active mining area from the leased commercial space [4].

## 4. Layout & Architecture

### 4.1 The grid

SubTropolis is a strict orthogonal grid of **16-ft (4.9 m) tall** × **40-ft (12 m) wide** tunnels separated by **25-ft (7.6 m) square limestone pillars**, generated by the classic **room-and-pillar** hard-rock mining method [1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 12]. The corridor grid runs in straight, named streets — no winding caverns, no natural cave geometry. Per Wikipedia (citing the *Atlantic*'s Steve Nadis), the streets are named for **geologic layers of limestone or shale** (e.g., one of the named streets is "Hushpuckney," named after a local rock type) and the pillars are individually numbered [1, 6, 15]. A few sources (the Stolen History wiki) use slightly different numbers: "25-foot square pillars on 65-foot centers and 40 feet apart" — the same dimensions, just clarifying that 40 ft is the pillar-to-pillar clear spacing [16].

The Hunt Midwest TripAdvisor listing (as of late 2020s) gives the most detailed operational spec sheet found anywhere [8]:

| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Leasable space | > 6,500,000 sq ft |
| Expansion capacity | > 7,500,000 sq ft |
| Lighted paved roads | 8.2 mi |
| Railroad track | 2.1 mi |
| Truck dock locations | > 500 |
| Truck lines serving site | > 300 |
| Companies | 55+ international, national, regional, local |
| Employees on site | > 2,000 |
| Parking spaces | > 1,600 |
| Limestone pillars | > 10,000 |
| Compressive strength of roof/pillar rock | 18,000–24,000 psi (≈ 6× concrete) |
| Total mined footprint | > 55,000,000 sq ft |

Wikipedia currently gives a smaller road figure — "almost **10.5 mi (16.9 km)** of illuminated, paved roads and several miles of railroad track" — and is broadly consistent with the TripAdvisor 8.2 mi [1, 6]. Other sources give 10.5 mi (Wikipedia/Metro/STOL) or 7–7.8 mi (The Drive, Stolen History) [5, 7, 10, 16]. **A 2026 Brazilian engineering press release** cites "21 kilometers [~13 mi] of streets" [7]. The variance appears to be a mix of "paved corridor" vs. "all drivable internal road including spurs," and the Hunt Midwest current site (10 M sq ft developed since 1964) seems to favour the 8.2 mi number for paved arterial roads plus several miles of rail spur [3, 8].

### 4.2 Floor, ceiling, lighting, ventilation

- **Ceiling**: flat 16 ft (4.9 m) clearance in the standard corridors; some newer chambers (e.g., the data-center / Tech Center build-outs) reach 17–20 ft clear height. Currently marketed warehouse spaces list 14 ft, 17 ft, or higher clear heights [10, 17].
- **Floor**: in standard warehouse corridors, poured and polished concrete; older corridors may have rough limestone floor. TripAdvisor: "concrete flooring" in the tunnels [7, 8].
- **Walls and ceiling**: raw excavated limestone — in some places painted white, in others raw honey-colored to grey limestone with visible tool marks. According to the TripAdvisor copy, exposed rock is part of the visual character [8].
- **Lighting**: continuous overhead fluorescent / LED strips; the corridors are described as "fully dry, fully lit, paved streets" [6, 8, 15].
- **Ventilation**: drive-in entrances act as fresh-air intakes. Truck idling inside the facility is **prohibited** to prevent the buildup of exhaust fumes [1, 6, 7]. Wikipedia notes that AM-FM radio signals do not penetrate the rock (a separate "above-ground receiver" is required for any radio-equipped tenant) [1, 6].
- **Fire protection**: the entire complex is protected by fire sprinklers [8].

### 4.3 Above-ground infrastructure

- The Hunt Midwest / SubTropolis operation has developed a separate surface landholding above the mine: vehicle auction and storage yards, trucking terminals, the Worlds of Fun / Oceans of Fun amusement park complex, and parking for 1,600+ cars [2, 4, 8]. The 2,500 surface acres are mostly Hunt-owned even after the 1995 Worlds-of-Fun sale (Hunt retained mining and real-estate rights) [4, 6].
- According to the Axios 2026 Groundhog Run piece, **"the speed limit is set at 15 mph"** inside the mine — a deliberately low cap because the corridors are public drivable and shared with forklifts and trucks [15].

## 5. The Entrance & Access

### 5.1 Why "above-grade" entrances?

The defining spatial fact about SubTropolis is that the mined chambers lie *horizontally* into the bluffs above the Missouri River. There are no elevators, no vertical shafts, no cages. Trucks and cars **drive directly into the hillside** at street grade, which is one of the design reasons SubTropolis is cheaper to operate than deep-vertical-mining facilities like Louisville's Mega Cavern (which requires vehicle elevators) [1, 6, 7].

The Drive (2020): "the primary reason for choosing this location is that the mine is actually above ground, meaning traffic can go in and out of the SubTropolis without the use of a cumbersome mining elevator" [5].

### 5.2 The known entrance portals

The site is publicly addressable at **8300 NE Underground Drive, Kansas City, MO 64161** [10, 11, 16]. The road is a Hunt Midwest–owned private drive that leads from NE Parvin Road / NE 83rd Street down into the mouth of one of the principal portals. KCUR: the complex is **"open to the public — anybody can drive into one entrance of the mine and out another"** [5, 12].

Known entrance points:

1. **Main / administrative portal (south side)** — 8300 NE Underground Drive; this is the front-of-house address for Hunt Midwest Real Estate Development, the leasing office, and the property-management teams [10, 11].
2. **Derrough Drive entrance (newer, north side)** — opened as part of the newest phase (north of Derrough Drive); primary access from Parvin Road. Built to serve the 2012 NARA build-out and subsequent expansion including the 2026 Grainger project [12].
3. **The Drive** (2020) and YouTube tour videos note that **"there are several different entrance points"** — at least two named portals in regular use, with on-site map signage routing visitors between them [5, 7, 15].
4. **Surface parking and vehicle staging**: a dedicated surface lot with 1,600+ parking spaces is provided on the plateau above the mine, with truck and trailer staging areas for the 300+ trucking lines serving the dock network [4, 8, 12].

For the Minecraft replica, the entrance reads visually as **a horizontal portal in a hillside** — not a pit, not a shaft, not a cave mouth. The stone around the entrance is the same Bethany Falls limestone as inside; the road is a paved ramp at very shallow grade (the ULI study describes access "at street level through entrances in the sides of the hills") [4].

### 5.3 Signage and wayfinding inside

- Streets are named and signed — "Avenue" or numbered arterial names plus geology-themed sub-streets (Hushpuckney is one) [1, 6, 15].
- Pillars are individually numbered, by a numbering scheme tied to the block / cross-street [15, 16].
- Stop signs, lane markings, a 15 mph speed limit, and standard road signs are all in place, making the mine function legally as a private road network [15].
- No public transit, no rail passenger service. Workers arrive by car.

## 6. Tenants & Use Cases

SubTropolis advertises "55+ international, national, regional and local companies" and "2,500+ tenant employees" on its current site [3, 8]. Earlier (1998–2010) sources cite 50–55 tenants and 1,000–1,600 employees [4, 5, 6, 7]. The number of employees grew sharply in the 2010s with the data-center build-outs and NARA; the 2,000–2,500 figure is current (2024–2026) [3, 7, 8].

The tenant mix reflects what makes underground real estate uniquely valuable: stable climate, security, and low energy cost. Three categories dominate.

### 6.1 Federal government records & logistics

Per the 2012 Hunt Midwest press release, "in total, federal government tenants will occupy nearly one million square feet of space in SubTropolis" [12]:

| Tenant | Function | Approx. area |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **USPS Stamp Distribution Center** | Distributes stamps and packaging products to post offices | 217,114 sq ft [12] |
| **USPS Stamp Fulfillment Center** | Fulfills online orders for commemorative stamps, first-day covers, Forever stamps, etc. | 311,600 sq ft [12] |
| **EPA Region 7 Training & Logistics Center** | Emergency response operation | 43,200 sq ft [1, 6, 12] |
| **NARA Federal Records Center (Kansas City)** | National Archives & Records Administration federal records storage | 102,000 sq ft initial lease, options to 372,000 sq ft [1, 6, 12] |

USPS has been at the site since the 1960s. The 2012 NARA lease was the largest single federal move; the records center is "the first project located in the newest phase of SubTropolis north of Derrough Drive" [12]. The facility holds tax records, federal court documents, and other archival material that benefit from the constant temperature and humidity [1, 6, 7].

### 6.2 Commercial film & media archive

SubTropolis is the home of **Underground Vaults & Storage, Inc.** (UV&S), a "distributor of last resort" for the major American film studios. UV&S stores master film elements — including the negatives and interpositives of films such as *The Wizard of Oz* and *Gone With the Wind* — in SubTropolis's constant climate [1, 5, 6]. The CLUI entry (2011) lists UV&S as one of the "major commercial film archive" tenants alongside truck customization businesses, Hallmark Cards, and USPS [2].

### 6.3 Data centers (SubTropolis Technology Center)

The **SubTropolis Technology Center (STC)** is a separately marketed purpose-built data-center campus carved out of the same mine [1, 9]. Currently published spec (Hunt Midwest official site) [9]:

- Multiple Tier 1 providers: **AT&T, Spectrum Enterprise, Lumen**
- Dark-fiber connections to **1102 Grand** (the Kansas City carrier hotel at 1102 Grand Blvd)
- Biometric hand readers at tenant entry
- 24/7 armed on-site security and video surveillance
- ENERGY STAR 100 rated (2012 onward)
- LightEdge Solutions was the original anchor tenant; **W.W. Grainger's** expansion, slated for completion in **summer 2026**, will reportedly be the **largest known underground distribution center** in the world [1, 3, 6, 9].

### 6.4 Logistics, e-commerce, light industrial

Hunt Midwest's marketed spaces today include buildings such as [10]:

- **Building 86A2** — 108,000 SF, 17 ft clear height, 10 dock doors
- **Building 92A** — 62,200 SF, 17 ft clear height, 14 dock doors
- **Building 73A** — 199,405 SF, 14 ft clear height, 20 dock doors

Historically and currently: light manufacturing, e-commerce fulfillment, food packaging, pharmaceutical distribution, auto customization, vehicle storage (Ford's 25-acre lease, historically; replaced in scale by Grainger today), industrial warehousing for companies like Hallmark, Russell Stover, and Pillsbury (early 1960s onward) [1, 2, 5, 6, 7].

### 6.5 Foreign-trade zone

Hunt Midwest's 2012 release notes SubTropolis is "the **largest foreign-trade zone under one roof in the United States**" [12] — meaning a substantial portion of the leasable space is designated FTZ, allowing tenants to defer or免除 duties on stored imports.

### 6.6 What a visitor would actually see

A 2020 YouTube drive-through [5] describes:

> "A 150 ft beneath Kansas City road system with street signs, traffic rules, and a 15 mph speed limit. … Only a fraction of the 55 million square ft of mined out space has been developed, and the mining never fully [stops]."

A 2026 *Axios Local* reporter [15] writing about the Groundhog Run:

> "Enormous 25-foot-wide stone columns support the ceiling, each marked with a number. 'Street' names feature Hushpuckney, named after a local rock type. The speed limit is set at 15 mph. Dark, unlit tunnels obstructed by barriers and debris fade into pitch-blackness."

> "Anyone can drive into one entrance of the mine and out another."

A 2024 *Metro* (UK) feature [7]:

> "It's accessible from street level via a number of different entrances — and the curious can also drive in and take a look around."

## 7. Climate & Conditions

This is the single most-cited reason SubTropolis works as a business location.

- **Year-round air temperature**: **65–70 °F (18–21 °C)** [1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12]. TripAdvisor and the Kansas City Business Journal call it "near-constant temperature" [8, 12]. The *Metro* and *Drive* articles call it specifically "70 °F year-round" and "68 °F" respectively [5, 7].
- **Humidity**: stable; "no humidity" per The Drive [5]. NARA chose the site in part because of "constant temperature and humidity levels" needed for archival storage [12].
- **No weather**: no rain, snow, ice, wind, sun, UV, or temperature swings [1, 6, 7]. "Protected from natural disasters and severe weather" per Hunt Midwest's current marketing [3].
- **No pests / no insects / no rodents** because of the sealed rock envelope [1, 6].
- **No AM/FM radio** — signals do not reach inside; tenants who want radio must install above-ground receivers [1, 6].
- **No sunlight** — workers report a kind of "mole people" lifestyle; one 1998 *Asian Wall Street Journal* story was headlined "Between a Rock and a Hard Place Toil the Mole People of Kansas City" [1, 6].
- **No truck idling** (to control exhaust buildup) [1, 6, 7].
- **Truck exhaust and ventilation** are managed through the street-level portals acting as passive intakes plus mechanical supply [1, 6].

### 7.1 Energy economics

Multiple sources give slightly different numbers for the energy savings; the variance is partly real (different tenant fit-outs have different baselines) and partly a marketing claim:

- Hunt Midwest press (2012): "low utility costs that offer **50–70 % savings in total energy costs**" [12].
- TripAdvisor (Hunt's own listing, current): "Up to 90 % savings in total energy costs" [8].
- Bloomberg / Drive (2015/2020): rent is $2.25/sq ft; energy is a major component of savings [5].
- Climate control alone is reduced "by as much as 85 %" per various magazine features [1, 6].

The site's **EPA ENERGY STAR** score is **100 (perfect)** in the unrefrigerated warehouse category — held every year since 2012, when it became the only facility in Kansas or Missouri to receive a perfect score in that category [1, 3, 6, 12].

### 7.2 Structural & safety characteristics

- Compressive strength of roof/pillar rock: 18,000–24,000 psi (~6× concrete) [8, 12].
- Fire sprinklers throughout the developed space [8].
- Natural isolation → no pest control needed; reduced insurance premiums for stored goods [1, 6].
- The 2001 USPS anthrax scare provided an unexpected "shelter-in-place" stress test: the underground facility is essentially a sealed environment that filtered the building's internal air through HEPA-grade intake [1, 6].

## 9. Visual Description

What a visitor would see in person, based on a synthesis of drive-through videos, the *Atlantic* piece, the *Axios* and *Metro* reportage, the CLUI photo, and the Hunt Midwest marketing photos [2, 5, 6, 7, 15]:

- **The surface entrance**: a small modern office / portal building set into a tree-covered bluff. A standard asphalt road slopes gently down into a tunnel mouth roughly the height of a single semi-truck. Signage reads "SubTropolis" and the Hunt Midwest logo. The road inside is a normal two-lane paved road with painted lane lines.
- **The interior "highway"**: roughly 40 ft wide (≈ 3 lanes) with a smooth concrete or polished-asphalt driving surface. White-painted limestone walls, low ceiling with continuous fluorescent strip lighting. Road signs, stop signs, painted lane lines, a 15 mph speed limit.
- **The pillars**: regular, square, roughly 25 ft on a side, between every junction. The pillars are *unpainted honey-grey limestone*, with visible drill marks and tool scoring in the upper parts where the mine originally cut. Some pillars are painted (e.g., white, or a tan color) in the more polished areas.
- **The ceiling**: flat, 16 ft high, the underside of the next limestone bed, mostly left rough but painted white or light grey in the developed corridors.
- **Tenant fit-outs**: industrial steel mezzanines, dock doors, white box office partitions, and HVAC ducts form a "second city" inside the rock. The aesthetic is half industrial warehouse, half underground parking garage.
- **Lighting**: a mix of overhead industrial fluorescents and tenant fit-out fixtures. The corridors are bright; the deep undeveloped chambers fade to black.
- **The sound**: quiet. Low traffic hum, HVAC, the occasional forklift. The video testimonials describe it as "spooky but cool" [5, 7].
- **Atmosphere**: cool, dry, slightly mineral. Workers report it takes about 15 minutes to acclimate; some people get headaches the first few times because of the lack of UV and the constant 65–70 °F.
- **The exit**: a second portal somewhere else in the same bluff, opening back out onto another surface road. From inside, you may not be able to tell when daylight is just around the next curve; the transition is dramatic [5, 7, 15].

**Color palette** (for the Minecraft build):
- Limestone walls: pale honey-amber to light grey, with darker veining where the rock has more shale content
- Painted pillars and ceilings: white, off-white, or pale grey
- Floor: light grey (polished concrete) to medium grey (asphalt)
- Tenant fit-out: industrial greys, white walls, occasional safety yellow and red
- Lighting: warm white (~3000 K fluorescent) in older corridors, cooler (~4000 K) in newer fit-outs
- Accent colors on signs: standard road-sign greens and yellows

## 10. Minecraft Scaling Notes

The downstream design team will need to compress ruthlessly. At 1 block = 1 meter, the actual mine is unrenderable.

### 10.1 Raw scale math (1 block = 1 meter)

| Real-world metric | Real value | Blocks at 1:1 |
|-------------------|------------|----------------|
| Surface footprint | 1,100 acres = 4.5 km² | ≈ 2,100 m × 2,100 m square, unbuildable |
| Total mined volume | 55 M sq ft = 5.1 km² floor | 5,100,000 m² floor area |
| Leasable developed floor | 14 M sq ft = 1.3 km² | 1,300,000 m² floor area |
| Tunnel width | 40 ft = 12 m | 12 blocks wide |
| Tunnel height | 16 ft = 4.9 m | 5 blocks high |
| Pillar cross-section | 25 ft × 25 ft = 7.6 m × 7.6 m | 8 × 8 blocks |
| Pillar-to-pillar spacing | 65 ft center-to-center | 65 blocks |
| Corridor length (paved) | 8.2 mi = 13.2 km | 13,200 blocks |
| Depth below surface | 100–160 ft = 30–49 m | 30–49 blocks down |
| Compressive strength of rock | 18,000–24,000 psi | (n/a) |

**Conclusion**: A literal 1:1 build is impossible. Even at 1 block = 10 meters (10× compression), the surface footprint would still be 210 × 210 blocks and the corridor 1,320 blocks long — too sprawling for a buildable, walkable Minecraft site.

### 10.2 Recommended compression strategy

The build should treat SubTropolis as an *iconic microcosm*: a representative slice that captures the *character* of the place rather than every chamber.

- **Surface footprint**: compress to roughly **200 × 200 blocks** (about 1/10 of the real footprint at 1:1). Pick 6–10 representative corridors and leave the rest as "unfinished rock."
- **Vertical**: 5 blocks (16 ft) ceiling is a clean number; build to scale and the human-scale is preserved.
- **Pillar grid**: build a *few* intersections at true 25×25 pillar scale, then fake the rest with thinner pillars or rubble where "the mining hasn't reached yet."
- **Depth**: build the entrance portal at, say, Y=70 (the bottom of a 70-block-deep ravine — matching the "ravine/gorge" placement in the project brief). The corridor floor sits at the local sea level, with rock above and below.
- **Length of paved road**: build 200–400 blocks of "main avenue" as the iconic drive-through experience. Branch off into a couple of side spurs for distinct tenant types.
- **Surface above**: 1,600 parking spaces = ~3 acres of parking lot. A few representative parking rows + an "amusement park" stub (or a generic industrial surface development) is sufficient.

### 10.3 What MUST be reproduced (iconic non-negotiables)

Based on visitor reports and the Hunt Midwest brand, the items below are what makes SubTropolis *SubTropolis*:

1. **Horizontal portal in a hillside** (not a cave, not a pit, not a shaft). The visitor walks or drives a few blocks down a gentle ramp into the rock.
2. **Pillared corridor grid** with square cross-section pillars on a regular grid. This is the most iconic single image of the site.
3. **Street signs with geology-themed names** (Hushpuckney, etc.) and a **15 mph speed limit** posted.
4. **Painted pillar numbers** at every intersection.
5. **Continuous overhead lighting** in the main corridors.
6. **A multi-tenant mix visibly different from one block to the next** — e.g., a USPS loading dock next to a climate-controlled records vault next to a Hallmark ribbon-packing room.
7. **At least one data-center build-out** (the SubTropolis Technology Center) with biometric readers, dark-fiber conduit, and rack-cooling.
8. **A "ghost" of the un-built mine** — a couple of corridors deliberately left un-paved, dark, with raw rock, to convey that most of the 55 M sq ft is unfinished.
9. **A second portal** (the "drive out" exit) somewhere else in the ravine wall, to enable the open-public through-route.
10. **The constant climate** — convey this through game mechanics: no weather inside, low/no light from outside, a slight "muffled" effect on ambient sound, no mob spawning in the deep sections (a fun optional rule for the survival servers).
11. **Surface parking + an above-ground building** (the Hunt Midwest office / visitor center) at the portal mouth.
12. **A train / rail spur** in at least one chamber (the actual complex has "several miles" of rail; even one in-scale rail line is enough).

### 10.4 What can be safely elided

- The 2,500 surface acres (the amusement parks, vehicle auction, etc.) — a single block of "above-ground stuff" with maybe one tower / building is fine.
- The exact 55-tenant mix. Pick a representative 8–12 tenant types and label the rest with a "Vacant / Mining Crew" sign.
- The full 10.5 mi of road. 300–500 blocks of "main street" is enough to convey the scale.
- The 7.5 M sq ft of "expansion capacity" — leave a couple of unfinished chambers, mark them as off-limits, done.
- The 1,100-acre surface footprint. Compress to whatever fits the ravine.

### 10.5 Lighting and atmospheric tips for the builder

- Use **shallow lighting** (sea lanterns every 4–5 blocks, or glowstone at the ceiling every 6 blocks) to suggest the long fluorescent strips.
- **Wall texture**: the Bethany Falls limestone reads as a layered sedimentary rock. In Minecraft, layer **smooth stone + calcite + diorite + tuff** in roughly 70 / 15 / 10 / 5 % proportions; add occasional **deepslate** veins. The strata should be visible as horizontal bands.
- **Pillars**: 8×8 block, full-height, in **calcite or quartz block** (the lighter, more "limestone" feel). Top and bottom should match the rock above/below.
- **Floor**: 50/50 **polished andesite** (newer sections) and **stone** or **gravel** (older sections). Painted lane lines = black wool on light gray.
- **Sound**: in vanilla Minecraft, this means leveraging the existing ambient "cave" sound. The user feels a muffled step when walking on stone; that works.
- **Climate effect**: in vanilla, weather is global. To convey "no weather inside," build the SubTropolis such that the "outside" is at a much higher Y and the SubTropolis floor is below the build-protection of the ravine walls. The natural Minecraft "underground" effect handles this for you.

## 11. What a Minecraft Visitor Should Recognize

A first-time visitor to the build should walk away remembering these things (in order of importance):

1. **"I drove into a hill and there was an entire city under it."** This is the headline visual. The transition from bright outdoors to the dim, painted, fluorescent-lit grid of pillars is the single most memorable image.
2. **"It was bigger than I expected."** Use long sightlines down a single corridor with a 5-block ceiling and 8-block-wide pillar grid. The repetition *is* the impression.
3. **"There were office buildings and warehouses and loading docks underground, and they were using them for real things — mail, archives, server racks, vehicle storage."** Convey this with varied tenant fit-outs.
4. **"It was the same temperature everywhere and didn't smell like anything."** In Minecraft, this is a player observation: the build should feel mechanically distinct from "outdoor" — same ambient temperature, no weather, no mobs in the deep.
5. **"The streets had names like Hushpuckney and a 15 mph speed limit."** Custom sign posts with these names are an easy build detail.
6. **"There was a road that goes all the way through to another exit on the other side of the hill."** This is the iconic through-route. Build at least two portals on opposite (or perpendicular) sides of the ravine.
7. **"It was built by the same family that owns the Kansas City Chiefs."** Optional: name the central plaza / round-room "Hunt Hall" or place a Lamar Hunt bust somewhere as an easter egg.
8. **"There were parts that were still being mined, dark and unfinished."** A few dark side-chambers marked off with a "DANGER — Active Mining" sign make the build feel alive.
9. **"A train went through there."** At least one rail line in the main corridor with parked rolling stock.
10. **"They had a data center down there."** A locked, climate-controlled chamber with visible server racks and red/green status lights is a great visual hook.

If the build delivers those ten observations to a first-time visitor, it has captured the *SubTropolis* even if the footprint is a hundredth of the real one.

## 12. Sources & Confidence

### 12.1 What's confidently documented (multiple independent sources agree)

- **Identity of the site and its owner**: Hunt Midwest / Lamar Hunt, since 1964. (Wikipedia, ULI, *Atlantic*, CLUI, KCUR, *Drive*, Hunt Midwest own site, government press releases.)
- **Address**: 8300 NE Underground Drive, Kansas City, MO 64161. (Hunt Midwest, MapQuest, ULI, TripAdvisor.)
- **Carved into the Bethany Falls Limestone (~270 Ma)**. (Wikipedia, Hunt Midwest, ULI, ENR, TripAdvisor, *Atlantic*.)
- **Room-and-pillar grid: 16 ft × 40 ft tunnels, 25 ft square pillars on 65 ft centers**. (Wikipedia, Hunt Midwest, ULI, 2012 press release, *Atlantic*, *Drive*, *Metro*, *Stolen History*, TripAdvisor — all agree.)
- **Year-round temperature 65–70 °F**. (Hunt Midwest, Wikipedia, *Drive*, *Metro*, TripAdvisor, ULI.)
- **USPS as the longest-running marquee tenant**. (All sources.)
- **NARA Federal Records Center lease of 102,000 sq ft (with options to 372,000) in 2012**. (Hunt Midwest press release, Wikipedia.)
- **ENERGY STAR 100 warehouse rating**. (Hunt Midwest, 2012 press release, Wikipedia.)
- **First tenants 1964 = construction company, then Pillsbury, Russell Stover, Ford**. (Wikipedia, Hunt Midwest, *Atlantic*, Jalopnik/ENR.)
- **Open to the public, drive-through allowed**. (*Drive*, *Metro*, KCUR.)
- **2,500+ employees (current) / 1,600 (2015) / 1,000 (early 2000s) / 1,300 (~2000)**. (Multiple sources, with year markers.)

### 12.2 What is documented but with conflicting numbers (flag for downstream verification)

- **Total developed floor area**: 14 M sq ft is the most cited (Wikipedia, Hunt Midwest, ENR); some older sources say 4.5 M sq ft. The 4.5 M number appears to be the leasable-only figure from the late 1990s; 14 M is total developed area. Both are real numbers at different points in time.
- **Total mined volume**: 55 M sq ft is a marketing line (Hunt Midwest, *Drive*, KCUR). The leasable developed is 14 M of that. The 55 M is the void, not the build-out.
- **Road length**: 8.2 mi (TripAdvisor / Hunt) vs 10.5 mi (Wikipedia) vs 13 mi (2026 Brazilian) vs 7.8 mi (*Metro*). The paved-arterial figure is most likely 8.2 mi; the higher numbers include spurs and access roads.
- **Rail length**: 2.1 mi (TripAdvisor) vs "several miles" (Wikipedia) vs 21 mi (2026 Brazilian — almost certainly an error; maybe metric-to-imperial confusion with 21 km = 13 mi of *road*, not rail). Best estimate: 2–4 mi of rail spurs.
- **Temperature**: 65–70 °F (Wikipedia) vs 68 °F (*Drive*) vs 70 °F (*Metro*) — a 5-degree range. The mine isn't actively climate-controlled, so local temperature variations exist.
- **Energy savings**: 50–70 % (Hunt 2012) vs 85 % (Wikipedia) vs 90 % (Hunt 2026, TripAdvisor). 50–70 % is the more conservative number; 90 % is at the high end and probably includes all building systems.
- **Workforce**: 1,000 (CLUI 2011) / 1,300 (2000 per Wikipedia citing Asian WSJ) / 1,600 (2015 per The Drive) / 2,000 (2024 Wikipedia + current Hunt) / 2,500 (2026 Brazilian + current Hunt). The 2,000–2,500 number is current.
- **Ceiling height**: 16 ft (Wikipedia, Wikipedia citing old *Atlantic*) vs 17 ft (*Metro*). 16 ft is the standard corridor; some chambers go higher.

### 12.3 What's not directly confirmed (treat as approximate)

- The **specific names of streets** other than Hushpuckney. The 2010 *Atlantic* article and the 2026 *Axios* piece both reference street names themed on geology but the full list is not public.
- The **specific films held in the Underground Vaults & Storage archive**. *Wizard of Oz* and *Gone with the Wind* are widely repeated in secondary sources (Wikipedia, The Drive) but I did not find a primary source from UV&S confirming the exact inventory.
- The **25-acre Ford lease** (early 1970s) is in Wikipedia citing the *KC Public Library*; not independently confirmed in a Hunt Midwest primary source I could find.
- The **2,500 surface acres** vs the **1,100 underground acres** — the ULI 1998 case study gives the 2,500 figure; the math on the 1,100 is straightforward.
- The **exact cost savings** for the current Grainger expansion or the projected NARA expansion. Press releases confirm the projects; specific dollar figures are not yet public.

### 12.4 What's the right compressed mental model for the design team

- **Minecraft = ~ 1/100 the floor area** of the real site. Focus on a representative 6-block × 6-block chunk of the grid.
- **Vertical scale = 1:1** (the 16 ft / 5-block ceiling is the right proportion; don't compress this).
- **Pillars = 1:1** in the iconic intersections; thin or skip them in the unfinished zones.
- **Pillar grid spacing** can be relaxed to 30–40 blocks (one chamber every 3–4 pillars) without breaking the visual.
- **Length of road** is the most elastic dimension. A 200–400 block "main avenue" is more than enough to convey the through-route feel; 1,000+ blocks would just feel like a long highway, not a city.

## 13. Hand-off Notes to the Design Team

1. **The single most important visual is the entrance portal in the hillside.** Spend 80 % of the entrance build on getting this right: paved ramp, signage, transition from daylight to fluorescent, the first view of the pillar grid through the tunnel mouth.
2. **The second most important visual is the pillar grid in profile.** From across a wide corridor, looking sideways, the eye should see a regular 8×8 column row of pale limestone vanishing into the distance.
3. **The third most important is the tenant mix.** A corridor that goes "USPS loading dock → NARA archive → Grainger fulfillment → LightEdge data center → Hallmark packaging → Vacant / Mining Crew" is the whole story.
4. **For the ravine / gorge context** (since the project brief places SubTropolis on the south side of a deep ravine in the same mountain range as Cheyenne Mountain): the portal should be roughly at the *bottom* of the visible ravine wall, with a paved road winding down from a surface plateau. Above the portal: tree-covered bluff, maybe a small visitor center, the parking lot, and the view out across the gorge.
5. **Anti-pattern to avoid**: a single huge cavern. SubTropolis's visual character comes from the *regular orthogonal grid*, not from a big open room. If the build starts to feel like "a big underground room," it's wrong; it should feel like "a city street grid with no sky."
6. **For the merge map**: when this combines with Cheyenne Mountain and the Houston tunnel system into a single "merged complex" report, SubTropolis provides the only *commercial / civilian* use case. The downstream map-integration report should treat it as the "open to the public, mixed use" leg, sitting visually distinct from Cheyenne Mountain (military, secure, single-use) and the Houston tunnel system (pedestrian, downtown). SubTropolis = the "suburban industrial" leg of the trilogy.
